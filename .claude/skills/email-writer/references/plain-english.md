# Plain English, short paragraphs, and the parent twin

Waleed's two notes from 13 September 2026, verbatim in substance: Hormozi's and Suby's emails use shorter paragraphs because "large chunks of writing feel like a bit of a commitment", and "a lot of the parents that I speak to, their first language isn't even English, so we need to make sure that it's easy for them to understand."

## The bar (measured by `scripts/readability.py`)

- Paragraphs: one to three sentences, most one or two. Average 23 words or fewer. Nothing over 45 (Waleed flagged 45+ word paragraphs as still too long on 13 September; the approved Sunday issue averages 21). A very short paragraph of quick beats or quoted speech (25 words or fewer) may run to four sentences, and the PS may run to 55 words because it stays one block (the approved Sunday issue's PS is 53).
- Sentences: average 13 words or fewer. Nothing over 30. If a sentence has two commas and an "and", split it.
- Flesch reading ease 85 or above (about reading age 9). The 13 September issue scored 91.
- Bold step headings on their own line, so the eye can skim the structure.
- One idea per paragraph. If a paragraph changes subject halfway, that is two paragraphs.

## Words

- Short and common: "use" not "utilise", "help" not "assist", "plan" not "strategise". Contractions always.
- Numbers as digits inside the teach ("a 6 mark question gets about 7 minutes"); written words for small counts in prose ("two or three timed papers").
- Say the same thing the same way every time. Once the idea is called "time per mark", it is never "your ratio" or "the figure" later.
- British English (revise, marks, Year 13, A-level, maths).

## Idioms and phrases that trip non-native readers (swap on sight in parent emails; use sparingly with students)

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| just around the corner | coming soon |
| turning into a row | turning into an argument |
| look at you blankly | aren't sure what you mean |
| get ahead of this | sort this out early |
| on the fence | not sure yet |
| a decent chunk | a large part |
| in the long run | over time |
| bear with me | please be patient with me (students can keep "bear with me", he uses it himself) |
| sort a time | find a time |
| keep an eye on | watch |
| it clicks | it makes sense |
| pull it off | manage it |
| off the mark | wrong |
| a head start | an early advantage (or keep "head start", it is common enough for students) |

Keep his own everyday phrases that read fine in plain English: "no one size fits all", "I see it all the time", "it's just good maths", "blame the night shifts haha".

## The parent twin

Every email to "the whole list" is two emails. The parent twin is not a find-and-replace of "you" with "your child":

- Same teach, same steps, same idea name, so a parent can talk to their child about it in the same words.
- Reframed as something to pass on at home: "here are three simple steps to share with your child."
- Add the one thing a parent can do: a better question to ask at home ("What's your time per mark for that paper?") in place of "have you revised?", or a way to bring it up without a fight. Forwarding the email to the child is a valid action.
- The offer is "a free academic strategy call for your child", the qualifier is "parents who want their child to achieve top grades", the ask in return is "keep me updated on how things go", followed by "I'm confident we can help."
- Register is the Sonia email: `Hi {$name},` "I hope you're well!", "Best wishes,". The night shifts joke stays in the PS (Waleed's instruction). Smileys stay rare.
- Footers and recipient groups come from the audience in the manifest; the parent twin never goes to a student group and no student email ever goes to a parent group.
