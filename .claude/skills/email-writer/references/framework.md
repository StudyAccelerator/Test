# The framework: eight parts, every email

Built from the 13 September 2026 Sunday Session ("marks per minute"), which Waleed approved after one round of his own notes. The full approved text is in `content/email-newsletter/2026-09-13-marks-per-minute.md` (students) and `2026-09-13-marks-per-minute-parents.md` (parents). Read both before writing; they are the pattern.

Not every email carries every part (a two-paragraph daily has no numbered steps), but the order never changes, and parts 1, 7 and 8 are always there.

## 1. The warm opener (two short paragraphs)

Students: `Hey {$name},` then `I hope you're well and you've had a good weekend, it's Dr Waleed!` (vary the middle clause to the day: "I hope the first week back has been kind to you"). Parents: `Hi {$name},` then `I hope you're well and you've had a lovely weekend! It's Dr Waleed.`

Inside a sequence, only the first email introduces him ("Dr Waleed here."). Later emails open `Hi {$name},` and go straight in. The Sunday Session then names itself: "Welcome to this week's Sunday Session. It's the email where I share one thing school never really teaches you, that you can use straight away."

## 2. Why this matters now (one or two paragraphs)

Timestamp the email in the A-level year and say plainly why the reader should care this week. "You're probably a couple of weeks into the new school year now. That means your first proper tests are coming up soon. And if you're in Year 13, those early tests matter even more. Your teachers use them as evidence when they set your predicted grades, so every mark counts."

Waleed's note on this: he loved having the reason before the advice. Never open on the advice.

## 3. The problem, as he sees it (one or two paragraphs)

"I see it all the time. A student knows the content, sits the paper, and the last 6 mark question is still blank when time runs out. It almost never happens because they're slow. It happens because nobody ever showed them how to plan their time."

Concrete, one specific student picture, and the cause is always "nobody showed them", never "they're lazy".

## 4. The medical comparison (optional, one per email)

"Now, as a doctor, I think about exams a lot like triage in A&E. When the department is busy, you don't spend forty minutes on a sprained ankle while someone with chest pain is waiting."

His own pattern from the E0 ("just like in healthcare, the diagnosis isn't the only important bit"). Use it when it makes the idea easier to understand, not as decoration. Diagnosis vs management, triage, high-yield vs low-yield, "diagnose before you treat" are the ones he uses. One per email, never two.

## 5. The teach (numbered bold steps)

Each step heading is bold and sits on its own line: `**Step 1: Work out your time per mark.**` Then one to three short sentences per paragraph, with a worked example that uses real numbers ("a 2 hour paper worth 100 marks gives you 1.2 minutes per mark").

Give the idea a memorable name. Waleed rejected "your number" as vague and asked for "time per mark"; the name is then used identically in every step and in the parent question, so it sticks.

End the teach with a reassurance that it gets easier ("after two or three timed papers it becomes automatic") and one line of his humour where it fits ("it isn't giving up, it's just good maths!").

## 6. Tonight, try this (one paragraph)

`**Tonight, try this:**` followed by one action that takes ten minutes or less, with the time named.

## 7. The bridge and the offer (four or five short paragraphs)

The bridge widens the teach to the reader's whole situation: "Timing is only one of the ways students lose marks they already know, though. For some, it's exam technique. For others, it's the way they revise, or a few topics that never quite stuck. The quickest way to find out which one is costing you the most is a custom academic strategy plan."

Then the offer, in his order:

- **The reward:** "So as a thank you for being part of the Sunday Session, I'd love to offer you a completely free academic strategy call." (In sequences: "as a reward for taking the diagnostic and taking an active approach to your grades".)
- **What the call delivers:** "It's 20 to 30 minutes with me. I'll show you exactly what's holding you back, tell you what I'd change first, and give you a clear plan for the rest of this term."
- **The ambition qualifier and the ask in return:** "I keep a small number of these each week for students keen to get top grades this year. All I ask in return is that you keep me updated on the improvements you see :)"
- **The proof line:** "I've worked with over 1,000 students in the past 6 years. And I promise, not a single student has ever walked out of the strategy call thinking it wasn't worth their time." (For programme pitches: "on average, our students jump two grades", a real quote, then the satisfaction guarantee.)
- **The link, as named text on its own line:** `[LINK: Click here to book your free academic strategy call -> https://scheduler.zoom.us/dr-waleed-ahmad/academic-strategy-call]`

Parents get "I'm confident we can help." after the ask in return (his Sonia line).

## 8. Sign-off and PS

Students: `Speak soon!` then `Kind regards,` then `Waleed`. Parents: `Best wishes,` then `Waleed`. Sequence teaching emails may close `Keep going!` then `Waleed`. The engine lifts the valediction into the photo signature block, which sits last, after the PS.

**PS variety (Waleed, 14 September 2026).** A subscriber reads the whole journey, so before writing any PS, read the PSs of the emails around it, including the ON instant and 6 hour emails that run before day 1. Never repeat a joke or device within a journey: the night-shift/slot-times joke at most once (the day 1 email or earlier owns it), "I read every reply" at most once, "between shifts/patients" at most once, and never the same promise twice ("the best 20 to 30 minutes..."). Most PSs are deposits in Ian Stanley's sense: a value add, a confession, a one-line tip, a reply prompt with a reason. The call link rides in the PS only where the body doesn't already carry it, roughly every third email. Waleed's own slot-explainer PSs (why the calendar can look empty, how slots are released against his rota) are the reference for making the doctor's life useful rather than decorative.

The PS always exists, restates the one action, and carries his real life plus the link again: "PS: I'm a full time doctor, so the slot times might look a bit strange some weeks (blame the night shifts haha). Just book whichever day and time works best for you. I promise it'll be the best 20 to 30 minutes you spend on your grades this term. [Click here to book](url)". Waleed asked for the night shifts joke in the parent version too, so parents get it as well.

## Length

The Sunday Session runs 650 to 750 words. Sequence teaching emails 350 to 600. Dailies 150 to 350. Shorter is fine; longer needs a reason.
