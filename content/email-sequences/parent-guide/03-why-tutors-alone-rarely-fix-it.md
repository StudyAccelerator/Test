When a hard-working child's grades disappoint, most families reach for the same fix: a tutor. It's the industry default, it's what everyone at the school gate did, and at around £50 an hour it's a serious commitment. So before you make it, or renew it, here's the honest version of what tutoring can and can't do, from someone who runs live teaching for a living and will happily tell you when not to buy any.

**What a good tutor genuinely fixes:** content gaps. A topic never understood properly, a foundation missing from GCSE, a syllabus section the school rushed. If the problem is "they don't understand it", an hour with a good explainer is exactly the right medicine.

**What a tutor structurally can't fix:** what happens in the other 165 hours of the week. If your child learns a topic beautifully on Tuesday and revises it by re-reading, it fades by Friday and resets by the next test. The tutor explains it again next week, everyone feels progress, and the cycle quietly bills £200 a month for treading water. Not because the tutor is bad, but because the leak isn't in the understanding, it's in the method the understanding gets poured into. I watch families run this loop for two years, changing tutors, when the tutor was never the broken part.

**The order that works:** diagnose first, then fix the method if the method is the leak, and then tutoring, if it's still needed, actually sticks, because the hours between sessions finally hold what the sessions build. Sometimes the diagnosis really is a content gap, and then a tutor, or our subject programmes, is the right first move. That's what diagnosis is for. Guessing at £50 an hour is the only option I'd rule out.

If your child already has a tutor, nothing here says stop. It says check what the tutor is being asked to fix, and whether it matches what's actually broken.
