When a hard-working child's grades disappoint, most families reach for the same fix: a tutor. It's the default, it's what everyone at the school gate did, and at around £50 an hour it's a serious commitment. So before you make it, or renew it, here's the honest version of what tutoring can and can't do, from someone who runs live teaching for a living and will happily tell you when not to buy any.

**What a good tutor genuinely fixes:** content gaps. A topic never understood properly, a foundation missing from GCSE, a section the school rushed. If the problem is "they don't understand it", an hour with a good explainer is exactly the right medicine.

**What a tutor can't fix:** what happens in the other 167 hours of the week. If your child learns a topic beautifully on Tuesday and revises it by re-reading, it fades by Friday and it's gone by the next test. The tutor explains it again next week, everyone feels like progress is happening, and the arrangement quietly costs £200 a month for standing still. Not because the tutor is bad, but because the problem was never the understanding. It was the method the understanding gets poured into. I watch families run this loop for two years, changing tutors along the way, when the tutor was never the broken part.

**The order that works:** diagnose first. Then fix the method, if the method is the problem. And then tutoring, if it's still needed, actually sticks, because the hours between sessions finally hold what the sessions build. Sometimes the diagnosis really is a content gap, and then a tutor, or our subject programmes, is the right first move. That's what diagnosis is for. The only option I'd rule out is guessing at £50 an hour.

If your child already has a tutor, nothing here says stop. It says check what the tutor is being asked to fix, and whether that matches what's actually broken.
