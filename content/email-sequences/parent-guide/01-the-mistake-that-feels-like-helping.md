Hi {$name},

Today I want to share the most common mistake I see in homes with an A-level student. You've probably made it this week. Most loving parents have, mine included.

It took me years to work out why it doesn't work.

**The mistake is the question "have you revised?"**

It feels like being involved, and it's asked with love. But here's what it really does. It asks for a yes, so it gets a yes.

"Yeah, I did some earlier." The conversation ends there. You know nothing more than you did before.

And your child has just had another practice run at making revision sound like it happened. Ask it every day and it becomes background noise. It changes nothing except the mood in the kitchen.

**It measures the wrong thing**

"Have you revised?" counts time in the chair. But two hours of re-reading notes produces almost nothing except the feeling of having worked.

Twenty honest minutes of self-testing produces real memory.

So a child can truthfully say yes every single day while their method wastes most of the effort. The effort was never the thing that needed watching. The method was.

**The swap: ask about the process, not whether it happened.**

"What did you work on today?" opens a real conversation.

"Which topic scares you most right now?" gets you real information. And they name it themselves, which matters.

And the best one, used now and then: "How do you check it actually stuck?"

If the answer is any version of "I go over it again", you've just heard the real problem. You've learned more in ten seconds than a month of "have you revised?" would tell you.

**Tonight, try this**

Ask one process question in place of the usual one. Casually, once, and then leave it there.

You're changing from someone who checks up on them to someone on their side. Teenagers can feel the difference within days.
