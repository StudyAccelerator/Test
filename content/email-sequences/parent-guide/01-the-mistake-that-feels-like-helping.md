Today, the most common mistake I see in households with an A-level student. You've probably made it this week. Most loving parents have, mine included, and it took me years to see why it backfires.

The mistake is the question "have you revised?"

It feels like engagement. It's asked with love. And here's what it does in practice: it asks for a yes, so it gets a yes. "Yeah, I did some earlier." The conversation ends, you're none the wiser, and your child has just practised the small art of making revision sound like it happened. Ask it daily and it becomes wallpaper: a question that changes nothing except the temperature of the kitchen.

Worse, it measures the wrong thing. "Have you revised" counts time in the chair. But two hours of re-reading notes produces almost nothing except the feeling of having worked, and twenty honest minutes of self-testing produces actual memory. A child can truthfully answer yes every single day while their method quietly wastes most of the effort. Effort was never the thing that needed watching. The method was.

**The swap: ask about the process, not the fact of it.** "What did you work on today?" opens an actual conversation. "What's the topic that scares you most right now?" gets you real intelligence, and they name it themselves, which matters. And the best one, used sparingly: "how do you check it actually stuck?" If the answer is any version of "I go over it again", you've just heard the leak out loud, and you've learned more in ten seconds than a month of "have you revised" would tell you.

Tonight's move: one process question in place of the usual one. Casually, once, then let it breathe. You're switching from inspector to ally, and teenagers can feel the difference within days.
