Today, the most common mistake I see in households with an A-level student. You've probably made it this week. Most loving parents have, mine included, and it took me years to work out why it backfires.

The mistake is the question "have you revised?"

It feels like being involved, and it's asked with love. But here's what it actually does: it asks for a yes, so it gets a yes. "Yeah, I did some earlier." The conversation ends, you know nothing more than you did, and your child has just had another practice run at making revision sound like it happened. Ask it daily and it becomes background noise, a question that changes nothing except the mood in the kitchen.

Worse, it measures the wrong thing. "Have you revised" counts time in the chair. But two hours of re-reading notes produces almost nothing except the feeling of having worked, and twenty honest minutes of self-testing produces actual memory. So a child can truthfully answer yes every single day while their method wastes most of the effort. The effort was never the thing that needed watching. The method was.

**The swap: ask about the process, not whether it happened.** "What did you work on today?" opens an actual conversation. "Which topic scares you most right now?" gets you real information, and they name it themselves, which matters. And the best one, used sparingly: "how do you check it actually stuck?" If the answer is any version of "I go over it again", you've just heard the real problem out loud, and you've learned more in ten seconds than a month of "have you revised" would tell you.

Tonight's move: one process question in place of the usual one. Casually, once, and then let it breathe. You're switching from inspector to ally, and teenagers can feel the difference within days.
