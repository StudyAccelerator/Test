"They revised it. Two weeks later it's like they never saw it." If that sentence has been said in your house, today's email explains it, because there's a hundred years of memory science behind it and knowing it changes what you push for.

Memory decays on a curve: steep at first, then flattening. A topic studied once is mostly gone within days. That's not your child's brain failing, it's every human brain's operating spec, mine and yours included. Think about how much of your last work training day you could write down now.

Here's the part schools rarely teach. The decay isn't defeated by studying harder the first time. It's defeated by returning to the material at the right moments: a quick self-test the day after, another a few days later. Each return, if it's done from memory with the book closed, resets the curve and flattens it. A topic returned to twice sticks for months. A topic studied once for twice as long doesn't. Timing beats intensity, and it isn't close.

This is why "they revised for hours" and "they remember nothing" are both true. The hours went in as one heroic block, the curve did what curves do, and by the test the account was empty. The fix costs about twenty minutes per topic, spread across a week, which is why the students who seem to remember everything often study fewer hours than yours does. They're not working harder. They're returning cheaper.

**What you can do with this from the kitchen:** when you hear about tonight's revision, ask one follow-up in a few days: "could you still do that thing from Tuesday, book closed?" Not as a test, as curiosity. If yes, the returns are happening. If the answer is a wince, your child just felt the curve personally, and feeling it once teaches more than being told a hundred times. Either way, you've just checked the machine rather than the effort, and the machine is the thing that decides the grades.

That's tonight's move: one curiosity question about last Tuesday's topic.
