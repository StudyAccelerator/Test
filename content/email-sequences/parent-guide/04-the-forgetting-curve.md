"They revised it. Two weeks later it's like they never saw it." If that sentence has been said in your house, today's email explains it, because there's a hundred years of memory research behind it, and knowing it changes what you push for.

Memory fades quickly at first, then more slowly. A topic studied once is mostly gone within days. That's not your child's brain failing, it's how every brain works, mine and yours included. Think about how much of your last work training day you could write down now.

Here's the part schools rarely teach. The fading isn't beaten by studying harder the first time. It's beaten by coming back to the material at the right moments: a quick self-test the day after, another a few days later. Each return, done from memory with the book closed, makes the topic fade more slowly. A topic returned to twice sticks for months. A topic studied once for twice as long doesn't. When you come back matters more than how hard you went the first time, and it isn't close.

This is why "they revised for hours" and "they remember nothing" can both be true. The hours went in as one big block, the fading did what fading does, and by the test there was nothing left. The fix costs about twenty minutes per topic, spread across a week. And that's why the students who seem to remember everything often study fewer hours than yours does. They're not working harder. They're coming back at the right times.

**What you can do with this from the kitchen:** when you hear about tonight's revision, ask one follow-up a few days later: "could you still do that thing from Tuesday, book closed?" Not as a test, as curiosity. If yes, the returns are happening. If the answer is a wince, your child just felt the forgetting for themselves, and feeling it once teaches more than being told a hundred times. Either way, you've just checked the method rather than the effort, and the method is what decides the grades.

That's tonight's move: one curiosity question about last Tuesday's topic.
