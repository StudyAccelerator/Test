The guide gave you the three questions. Yesterday's email gave you the process questions. Today, the tool that turns all those impressions into something you can act on: actual numbers.

My Revision Diagnostic is twenty questions about how a student really revises: where the hours go, what happens inside them, how material gets checked, what exams do to it all. It was built for students, and then so many parents asked to take it about their child that I built a parent version: same questions, worded for what you see from the outside. You don't need to know what's in their head, only what you see from the kitchen.

It takes about five minutes, and here's what comes back. A named profile, because the ways revision goes wrong are surprisingly few, and your child's will be one of them. An estimate of how many of their weekly study hours are currently going into low-yield work, which is the number that usually stops parents mid-scroll. Five scores showing which part of their system leaks most. And a specific recommended fix, matched to the diagnosis rather than to whatever I'd like to sell you, including free options.

Then there's the part I designed it for: the report is written to be read together. It says, with numbers, the thing your child most needs to hear from someone who isn't you: this isn't laziness and it isn't ability. It's the way they're revising, it's common, and it's fixable. A report can say that in a way a parent can't, because a report has no history in the argument.
