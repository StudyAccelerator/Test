Hi {$name},

Sometime in the next few weeks, there's a decent chance a test result comes home that neither of you wanted. Early autumn tests are where last year's method problems surface, and I'd rather you were ready for that moment than ambushed by it, because how the first ten minutes go decides a lot about the rest of the term.

Here's the thing to hold onto: a bad early test is data, not a verdict. Teachers use these tests to see where everyone stands after the summer. Predicted grades get set on the term's whole body of evidence, not on week three. And a poor mark in September, caught and understood, is close to the most useful thing that can happen, because it shows you exactly where the leak is while there's still a whole autumn to fix it.

But that's not how it feels to a 17 year old holding the paper. To them it feels like confirmation of the worst thing they privately believe about themselves. So the ten minutes matter.

**What makes it worse:** the sharp intake of breath. "But you revised for this?" (they hear: your effort is worthless). Comparing it to a sibling, a cousin, or your own school days. And launching into solutions before anyone has had dinner.

**What makes it better:** flatness first. "Okay. Annoying. What sort of questions lost the marks?" That single question moves the whole conversation from verdict to data, because bad marks always have a shape. Marks lost on things they'd never learned tell you one thing. Marks lost on things they knew but couldn't produce, or worded wrongly, or ran out of time for, tell you something completely different, and that second pattern is the most common and the most fixable one there is.

Then, ideally the next day, not in the moment: sit down together with the paper and sort the lost marks into "didn't know it" and "knew it but didn't score it". Twenty minutes. It turns a bad afternoon into the most precise diagnostic you'll get all term, better than anything I could measure from a questionnaire.

And say the sentence somewhere in there: "I know the effort was real." It costs nothing and it keeps the door open for everything else.
