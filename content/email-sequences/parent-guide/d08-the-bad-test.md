Hi {$name},

In the next few weeks, there's a good chance a test result comes home that neither of you wanted.

Early autumn tests are where last year's method problems show up. I'd rather you were ready for that moment than surprised by it. How the first ten minutes go decides a lot about the rest of the term.

**A bad early test is data, not the final word**

Teachers use these tests to see where each student is after the summer. Predicted grades get set on the whole term's work, not on week three.

A poor mark in September, caught and worked out, is close to the most useful thing that can happen. It shows you just where the leak is while there's still a whole autumn to fix it.

But that's not how it feels to a 17 year old holding the paper. To them it feels like proof of the worst thing they fear about themselves. So the ten minutes matter.

**What makes it worse**

The sharp breath in when you see the mark. "But you revised for this?" They hear: your effort is worthless.

Comparing it to a brother, a sister, a cousin, or your own school days. And jumping into fixes before anyone has had dinner.

**What makes it better**

Stay calm first. "Okay. Annoying. What sort of questions lost the marks?"

That single question moves the whole talk from blame to data, because bad marks always have a pattern.

Marks lost on things they'd never learned tell you one thing. Marks lost on things they knew but couldn't write down, or worded wrongly, or ran out of time for, tell you something else.

That second pattern is the most common and the most fixable one there is.

**The next day, try this**

Best done the next day, not in the moment: sit down together with the paper. Sort the lost marks into "didn't know it" and "knew it but didn't score it".

Twenty minutes. It turns a bad day into the most precise diagnostic you'll get all term, better than anything a quiz could measure.

And say this sentence somewhere in there: "I know the effort was real." It costs nothing, and it keeps them talking to you about all the rest.
