Tomorrow I'll send you the email about deciding. Today, one last useful thing first: what the twelve weeks actually feel like from inside, because "live weekly sessions" tells you the structure and nothing about the experience.

**Weeks one and two feel like relief.** Mostly because of what the sessions aren't: nobody re-teaches the textbook at you, and nobody gets put on the spot beyond what they volunteer. You work real exam questions with the mark scheme open, and the first time a specialist re-words an answer from "that's roughly right" to "that scores", something clicks that no amount of solo revision produces. Students usually leave session one slightly annoyed nobody showed them this earlier.

**Weeks three to five take the most effort.** The novelty's gone, the between-session practice is a real two to three hours, and this is where you find out the difference between liking the idea and doing the work. It's also where the recordings earn their keep when life collides with a weekend. I'd rather tell you this stretch exists than have it surprise anyone, because every programme worth doing has one.

**Around week four, the wording starts arriving on its own.** That's the consistent report: mark scheme phrases start turning up in answers without being summoned, because everyone's mistakes rhyme and you've now watched dozens of them corrected, your own included.

**Weeks six to ten settle into rhythm**, and this is where the timing pays off: it's deep autumn term, the school tests that feed predicted grades are happening, and the coached answers are going straight into them. The programme stops feeling like a thing on top of school and starts feeling like the training ground for it.

**Week twelve ends quietly**, which is how it should. No graduation ceremony, just a student who sits timed papers as a routine, self-marks harshly, and speaks mark scheme as a second language. The skills stay after the sessions stop, and that's the actual product.
