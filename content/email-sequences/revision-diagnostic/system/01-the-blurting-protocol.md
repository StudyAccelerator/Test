{$name},

Your report flagged {$diag_bottleneck|default('the way you revise')} as your biggest leak, so I want to start with the method that fixes it fastest. It's called blurting. You might have seen it on TikTok. Almost nobody does it properly, and doing it properly is the whole difference.

First, why it works, because you'll stick with a method you understand and drop one you're taking on faith.

When you re-read your notes, the content becomes familiar. And familiarity feels exactly like knowing it. Same warm feeling of "yes, I know this". But the exam never asks whether you recognise the cardiac cycle when you see it. It gives you a blank answer box and asks you to produce it from nothing. Recognising something and producing it are two completely different skills, and school only ever trained the first one. That's where "I revised loads and it went badly" comes from. It's not a knowledge problem, and honestly, it's not a you problem either. Nobody taught you this.

There's only one way to get better at pulling things out of your memory, and that's practising pulling things out of your memory. Blurting is exactly that, done properly.

**The protocol**

One: pick a single, specific topic. "Biology" is not a topic. "The cardiac cycle" is. Smaller than feels natural is about right.

Two: blank page, book closed, phone in another room. Write down everything you know about the topic. Definitions, diagrams, mechanisms, equations, half-remembered bits. It's meant to look messy. Speed matters more than neatness. Five to ten minutes.

Three: stop, open your notes, and check your page honestly. Tick everything you got right. Everything missing or wrong goes back on the page in a different colour. That second colour is the whole point of the exercise. It shows you exactly what you don't know yet, which is the one thing no revision guide can tell you.

Four: the part everyone skips. Close everything again and re-blurt just the second-colour material, from memory, right now. Not tomorrow. This is the step where "I saw the right answer" becomes "I can produce the right answer", and it only takes a few minutes.

Five: keep the page and date it. You'll come back to this topic tomorrow for ten minutes, and again in about four days. In a couple of days I'll show you how to schedule those returns so they actually happen, because there's a trick to it: your calendar does the remembering, not your willpower.

**The two rules that keep it honest**

Every session ends with the book closed. Whatever you studied, the last ten minutes are you writing out the core of it from memory. If you can't produce it now, three metres from your notes, you couldn't produce it in an exam hall in June, and it's better to find that out tonight.

And every wrong answer earns a line in an error log. One page per subject: the question, your wrong answer, the correct one next to it. Retest the log on Sundays. Wrong answers you've corrected are the highest-yield material you own, because each one is a mark you've already lost once.

That's it. No highlighters, no four-hour note-making sessions, no productivity apps. A pen, a messy page, and finding out what's actually in your head. That part is humbling, and it's also exactly why it works.

Tonight: one topic, one blurt, both colours, the second pass. Twenty-five minutes, all in. Then reply to this email with one word describing your first blurt page. Mine, at 17, would have been "humbling".

Keep going!
