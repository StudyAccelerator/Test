Let me tell you how doctors get examined, because when I sat my medical school finals I realised something that applies directly to your {$diag_worry_subject|default('priority subject')}.

In our practical exams, an examiner stands there with a checklist. Not a general impression of whether you seem like a good doctor, but a literal sheet: introduced themselves, washed hands, asked about pain, checked the pulse for long enough. You get the mark if you did the thing on the sheet, and you get nothing if you did something brilliant that isn't on the sheet.

So what do medical students do? We get hold of the checklists and we train to them. Out loud, over and over, with someone watching who knows the sheet and stops us every time we miss a line. It would be madness to revise for a checklist exam by reading a textbook and hoping. Everyone accepts this completely, because it's just how you prepare for an exam that's marked against set criteria.

Now here's the thing: your A-level is a checklist exam too. The mark scheme is the checklist. The examiner can only award what's on the sheet, no matter how much you clearly understand. And yet almost every A-level student prepares for it the madness way, reading the textbook and hoping, because nobody ever showed them that the sheet matters more than the impression.

You've spent this week learning to train to the sheet: the drill, the letters, the dictionary. That's you doing what every medical student does. The one piece that's hard to do alone is the person who knows the sheet, watching, and stopping you each time you miss a line. In medicine that's how the checklist becomes automatic, and it's exactly what the Subject Accelerator sessions are: a specialist with the scheme open, correcting your actual answers, week after week until the wording comes out right under pressure.
