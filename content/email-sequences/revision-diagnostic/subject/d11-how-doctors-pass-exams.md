Hi {$name},

Let me tell you how doctors get tested. When I sat my medical school finals, I saw something that applies straight to your {$diag_worry_subject|default('priority subject')}.

In our practical exams, an examiner stands there with a checklist. There's no vague sense of whether you seem like a good doctor.

There's a real sheet: introduced yourself, washed your hands, asked about pain, checked the pulse for long enough.

You get the mark if you did the thing on the sheet. You get nothing if you did something brilliant that isn't on the sheet.

So what do we all do? We get hold of the checklists and we train to them.

Out loud, over and over, with someone watching who knows the sheet and stops us each time we miss a line.

It would be mad to revise for a checklist exam by reading a textbook and hoping. Everyone knows this, because that's just how you train for an exam that's marked against a set list.

Now here's the thing: your A-level is a checklist exam too. The mark scheme is the checklist. The examiner can only give you what's on the sheet, no matter how much you clearly know.

And yet nearly all A-level students revise for it the mad way, reading the textbook and hoping. Nobody ever showed them that the sheet matters more than how good the answer sounds.

You've spent this week learning to train to the sheet: the drill, the letters, the dictionary. That's you doing just what we all did.

The one piece that's hard to do alone is the person who knows the sheet, watching, and stopping you each time you miss a line.

In medicine that's how the checklist becomes second nature.
