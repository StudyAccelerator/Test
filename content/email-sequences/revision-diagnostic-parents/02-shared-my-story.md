Hi {$name},

You've had five days of fairly practical emails from me, so today I want to tell you something more personal: why an NHS doctor spends his spare time teaching A-level students. It's a fair question, and the answer is where all of this comes from.

At A-level, I was the student your child might be. The hard worker. Hours in, notes immaculate, teachers reassuring my parents that I was diligent. And my results kept coming back lower than what I knew, and nobody, not one adult in the system, could tell me why. The only advice anyone had was "work harder", which for a student already working hard is somewhere between useless and unkind.

I made it to medical school anyway, and that's where I finally learned what had been wrong. Medicine forces the issue, because the volume of content is so large that studying inefficiently simply isn't survivable. So they teach you, or you discover, how learning actually works. Testing yourself instead of re-reading. Returning to material before it fades. Preparing for the exam that will actually be sat, against the criteria it will actually be marked on. Simple things, which could have been taught to 17 year old me in a fortnight, and never were.

That's the itch I've never stopped scratching. Over the last six years, alongside medicine, I've worked with more than 1,000 A-level students, and the pattern I see is almost always my own: a young person working genuinely hard, and all of it pouring into methods nobody ever checked. The waste of it still gets to me, and it's why the diagnostic exists, why these emails exist, and why so much of what I make is free.

So when I say I'm confident we can help {$diag_child_name|default('your child')}, that's where the confidence comes from. I've been the student, I've been the person who finally worked out what was wrong, and I've now watched it click for a great many families.

Best wishes,

Waleed
