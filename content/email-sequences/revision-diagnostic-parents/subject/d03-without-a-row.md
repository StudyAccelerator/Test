{$name},

The most common thing parents tell me after the diagnostic isn't a question about revision. It's this: "I agree with the report, but if I bring it up, it'll turn into an argument."

So today, the conversation itself. What six years of teaching teenagers, and a doctor's share of difficult conversations, have taught me about this exact chat.

**Don't open with the report.** A document with scores on it reads as evidence for the prosecution, even when it's on their side. Open with the thing the report actually says: "I know how hard you're working. I don't think the problem is effort, and apparently neither does the diagnostic."

That sentence matters because it's the opposite of what they're braced for. Most students with {$diag_child_name|default('your child')}'s profile privately suspect they're stupid or lazy. They're neither, the report says so with numbers, and hearing you say it first is worth more than anything else in this email.

**Let them find the leak themselves.** Show them the profile page and ask one question: "does this sound like you?" Then stop talking. The report is written in their language, it names the leak precisely, and self-recognition lands where lectures bounce. If they say "that's actually quite accurate", the conversation has already succeeded.

**Don't announce a regime.** The fastest way to kill a method change is to make it your project instead of theirs. One move at a time, and let the first one be small: the report's plan starts with ten minutes and a blank page.

**And if it goes badly anyway:** drop it and come back in three days. A shut-down conversation reopens. A forced one doesn't. There's no deadline on this that's worth the relationship.

That's tonight's move: the first sentence, said once, at a calm moment. Not the whole conversation. Just the door, opened.
