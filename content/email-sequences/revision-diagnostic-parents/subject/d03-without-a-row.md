The most common thing parents tell me after the diagnostic isn't a question about revision. It's this: "I agree with the report, but if I bring it up, it'll turn into an argument."

So today, the conversation itself. Here's what six years of teaching teenagers, and a doctor's share of difficult conversations, have taught me about this exact chat.

**Don't open with the report.** A document with scores on it feels like evidence against them, even when it's actually on their side. Open with the thing the report really says: "I know how hard you're working. I don't think the problem is effort, and apparently neither does the diagnostic."

That sentence matters because it's the opposite of what they're braced for. Most students with {$diag_child_name|default('your child')}'s profile privately worry that they're stupid or lazy. They're neither, the report says so with numbers, and hearing you say it first is worth more than anything else in this email.

**Let them find the leak themselves.** Show them the profile page and ask one question: "does this sound like you?" Then stop talking. The report is written in their language, it names the problem precisely, and people accept things they recognise in themselves far more easily than things they're told. If they say "that's actually quite accurate", the conversation has already worked.

**Don't announce a new regime.** The fastest way to kill a change like this is to make it your project instead of theirs. One move at a time, and let the first one be small: the report's plan starts with ten minutes and a blank page.

**And if it goes badly anyway:** drop it and come back in three days. A conversation that got shut down can be reopened. A forced one can't. There's no deadline here that's worth the relationship.

So that's tonight's move: the first sentence, said once, at a calm moment. Not the whole conversation. Just opening the door.
