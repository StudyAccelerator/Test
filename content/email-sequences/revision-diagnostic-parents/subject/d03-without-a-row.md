Hi {$name},

The most common thing parents tell me after the diagnostic isn't a question about revision. It's this: "I agree with the report, but if I bring it up, it'll turn into an argument."

So today is about the talk itself. Here's what six years of teaching teenagers, and a doctor's share of hard conversations, have taught me about this exact chat.

**Don't open with the report.**

A document with scores on it can feel like evidence against them, even when it's on their side. Open with the thing the report really says: "I know how hard you're working. I don't think the problem is effort, and neither does the diagnostic."

That sentence matters because it's the opposite of what they expect. Most students with {$diag_child_name|default('your child')}'s profile privately worry that they're stupid or lazy.

They're neither, and the report says so with numbers. Hearing you say it first is worth more than anything else in this email.

**Let them find the problem themselves.**

Show them the profile page and ask one question: "Does this sound like you?" Then stop talking.

The report is written in their language and it names the problem clearly. People accept things they see in themselves far more easily than things they're told. If they say "that's quite accurate", the talk has already worked.

**Don't announce a new set of rules.**

The fastest way to kill a change like this is to make it your project instead of theirs. One move at a time, and let the first one be small. The report's plan starts with ten minutes and a blank page.

**If it goes badly anyway, wait.**

Drop it and come back in three days. A talk that got shut down can be reopened, but a forced one can't. There's no deadline here that's worth the relationship.

**Tonight, try this:** the first sentence, said once, at a calm moment. You don't need the whole talk tonight, just the start of it.

I'll be back tomorrow with what other families have found. In the meantime, if the talk happens and surprises you either way, reply and tell me how it went. I read every one.

Best wishes,

Waleed

PS: The "does this sound like you?" question works on adults too, by the way. I've watched a few parents read their own teenage years in the report!
