{$name},

Yesterday I gave you a question to ask {$diag_child_name|default('your child')}: "when you revise something, how do you check it actually stuck?"

If you asked it, you got one of four answers. Here's how to read them, because each one points somewhere different.

**"I go over it again."** The most common answer, and the leak itself, said out loud. Re-reading builds familiarity, and familiarity feels exactly like knowledge from the inside, right up until an exam asks them to produce it from nothing. The move: don't correct them tonight. The report's plan does the correcting, one blank page at a time, and it lands better from the plan than from a parent.

**"I just know if I know it."** Confidence doing the job evidence should. Everyone's memory lies to them like this, adults included, which is a genuinely useful thing to say to them, because it makes the fix a human upgrade rather than a personal criticism.

**"I test myself."** Better than most. The follow-up that matters: "book open or closed?" Open-book testing is re-reading wearing a costume. If it's genuinely closed-book, the machine is better than the grades suggest, and the leak is probably in when they return to topics, which the report also measures.

**A shrug.** Not rudeness, usually. Nobody has ever asked them to have a method, so there's no answer to give. This is actually the easiest starting point of the four: nothing to unlearn.

Whichever you heard, the move is the same and it's small: the report's plan starts with ten minutes and a blank page. One topic, tonight or tomorrow, theirs to pick. The machine gets fixed one honest page at a time, not in one big talk.

Tomorrow I'll send you the evidence for what fixing it properly looks like. In the meantime, if the answer you got surprised you, reply and tell me what they said. I read every one, and I can usually tell you what it means.

Waleed
