# Short-form scripts, batch 1: the Viral Toolkit formats

Six filmable scripts, one per format supplied by Curtis Tilbury and Tom Natras (Viral Toolkit). Their structure is the skeleton for each one; the beat labels in [square brackets] are their words, so you can check every script against the format they gave. The writing, hooks and voice are ours.

**Hook alignment (checked 24 July against the Personal Brand Hub in Notion):** the hooks below are built on the patterns proven in the hub's reference material: the "it's not a problem with YOU, it's a problem with your TECHNIQUE" reframe (Curtis and Tom's retention intro, and the revelation beat in the Failing-to-1st Cambridge video), the "hours of revising vs the mate who crammed" comparison (the Angry Explainer's opener), the exam-blank felt moment ("you knew it yesterday, where's it gone"), backwards-counted lists that build tension, X to X transformation framing, and their rule that section titles intrigue without giving the technique away. Where a script's hook matches a specific reference, its "why this should hold" note says which.

**Short-form tracker references (watched 24 July, real numbers):** the four inspiration videos attached to the tracker's idea rows were pulled and watched frame by frame.

1. **Caption reel** (bella, Instagram, 127,946 likes): 7 seconds of aesthetic desk b-roll on loop, one static title on screen, all the value in a 10 point caption written in brain-science language. The video is just a shelf for the caption; saves and shares do the distribution.
2. **Ratings carousel** (stharzzzz, TikTok photo post, 84,700 likes, 34,704 saves): "Rate all the study methods that I use as a straight A student". Eight study photos, each a method with a score out of 10 and a blunt one line verdict. Scores blurting 1/10, which Waleed can answer.
3. **Red flag, green flag** (Tom Vorselen, the study coach, Instagram, 18,856 likes, 1,849 comments, 1:44): talking head, persistent RED FLAG / GREEN FLAG scoreboard, one habit per title card, instant verdict plus ONE tight science line, thumbnail files into its column.
4. **Bad / Good / Excellent apps** (sofieestudies, Instagram, 187,837 likes, 14 SECONDS): a three-column scoreboard (Bad, Good, Excellent), a category flashes up ("Studying", "Review", "Note-taking"), and three app icons drop into their slots as she names them. Roughly three words per item. No explanations at all. The highest performer of the four, and the shortest by a mile.

## The pacing rule (why this batch got rewritten on 24 July)

Waleed flagged the scripts as too long and not short-form enough, and the references prove him right. The 187K-like reel says its whole point in 14 seconds. The winners fire the verdict and move; they do NOT explain like a lesson. So every script below was cut to short-form pace using three rules:

1. **Front-load. No throat-clearing.** The first spoken line is the hook, never a run-up to it.
2. **One breath per beat.** Name the thing, give the verdict, give at most ONE short reason, move on. If a sentence has two "because" clauses, cut one.
3. **Let the screen carry the structure, the voice carries the energy.** Phase names, scoreboards and captions do the labelling so the talking stays fast.

The flow still has to sound like Waleed talking (that was the last note), so lines connect naturally, they're just shorter. Short-form tight, not robotic clipped. Read each aloud once and cut anything your mouth trips on.

**Audience:** students (Year 12 and 13). Parents will see them too; nothing here talks down to either.
**Next step per Curtis and Tom:** film one of each, then book the review call before posting.

## Production setup (applies to every script)

- Filmed on phone, low camera angle, static shot.
- Pen or pencil in hand. Notes, textbook, calculator around you, as if you were mid session and decided to film.
- Off the cuff energy: know the beats, not the exact words. If a line feels stiff out loud, say it your way.
- Scrubs: scripts 3, 4 and 5 lean on the doctor framing, so film those in scrubs. 1, 2 and 6 work best looking like a normal study session.
- Captions on everything (most viewers watch muted). On-screen hook text must be visible within the first second.

## Script 1: Long Listical

**Title for your own tracking:** "If I did A-levels again, the five phases"
**Length target:** about 90 seconds. This is deliberately the longest of the batch (Curtis and Tom spec the long listical around 2 minutes), but it's been trimmed so it moves. **Wardrobe:** normal clothes, at the desk.

**[Beat: proven hook]**

> I got into medicine with a revision method that was genuinely terrible. Took me years to realise the problem was never me, it was my technique. So if I sat my A-levels again, here's exactly what I'd do, in five phases... and phase five is the one nobody does, so stay for that.

*On-screen text: "If I sat my A-levels again: the 5 phases"*

**[Beat: list of ~5 linked points, framed as phases. First couple quick, detail saved for the last couple. Phase names kept intriguing rather than descriptive, per Curtis and Tom's vague-title rule.]**

> Phase one, diagnose. I'm a doctor, I can't treat a patient until I know what's wrong, and your revision's the same. So one hour, go through your spec, mark every topic red, amber or green. Be honest, because that list runs everything else.

> Phase two, first contact. First time you meet a topic, keep it quick. Understand it, rough summary, move on. Your first pass isn't where the marks are, so stop polishing your notes. Nobody's marking your notes.

> Phase three, the blank page, and this is where I went wrong at 17. I'd reread my notes until they felt familiar, but familiar is a trap. So instead: notes shut, blank page, write everything you can remember. Then open them and fix the gaps in a different colour. It feels harder because it is. That's where the marks are made.

> Phase four, too soon. Come back to each topic before you feel ready. Three days, then a week, then two. If going back feels comfortable, you've left it too long, because that little bit of forgetting is the actual exercise.

> And phase five, the one nobody does. Knowing a topic and scoring marks on it are different skills. So from halfway through the year: past papers, timed, no notes. Then mark your own answer against the mark scheme, line by line. You're learning what the examiner actually pays for... and the exam stops being a surprise.

**[Beat: close]**

> That's the system. Save it for September, and follow for the stuff school never taught you.

*On-screen text through the list: "PHASE 1: DIAGNOSE" / "PHASE 2: FIRST CONTACT" / "PHASE 3: THE BLANK PAGE" / "PHASE 4: TOO SOON" / "PHASE 5: THE PERFORMANCE", each appearing as you say it.*

**Caption:** If I had to sit my A-levels again, this is the whole system. Phase 5 is the one nobody does.
**Hashtags:** #alevels #alevelrevision #year12 #year13 #studytok

**Why this should hold:** the hook stacks the confession-with-authority opener and the "problem was never me, it was my technique" reframe both Cambridge reference videos are built on. The open loop about phase five pulls people through the quick early phases, and the phase names intrigue without giving the technique away. Trimmed hard so it moves; it's still the long one of the batch by design.

## Script 2: Short Listical

**Title for your own tracking:** "Habits that feel productive, ranked"
**Length target:** about 25 seconds. Very fast. Cut every pause in the edit. **Wardrobe:** normal clothes.

**[Beat: proven hook]**

> Five revision habits that feel productive, ranked by how much of your life they waste.

*On-screen text: "ranked by hours of your life wasted"*

**[Beat: fast list where each item builds tension. Each habit feels MORE like real work than the last. A couple of words each.]**

> Five, making the perfect timetable. I lost whole evenings to mine.

> Four, rewriting your notes in neat. That's art, not revision.

> Three, highlighting. The textbook looks amazing, your brain wasn't there.

> Two, rereading till it feels familiar. Familiar isn't knowing.

> And one, watching study videos instead of studying. Yeah. Including this one.

**[Beat: close]**

> And the scary bit? The higher up that list, the more it feels like real work. Test yourself instead. Follow, I'll show you how.

*On-screen text: countdown numbers 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 punching in with each item.*

**Caption:** Number one hurt to film. #alevels #alevelrevision #year12 #year13 #gcse

**Why this should hold:** counting backwards to one is the tension device the Angry Explainer reference uses (8 down to 1). Item one turns the camera on the viewer mid scroll (tag a mate who does this), and the closing reframe gives the list a point instead of a dunk. Already the tightest of the six; barely touched.

## Script 3: Quote Unpacking

**Title for your own tracking:** "If revision feels good"
**Length target:** 45 to 55 seconds. Slower pace than the listicals, but the middle got cut so it doesn't drift. **Wardrobe:** scrubs.

**[Beat: an interesting sentence where the meaning is not obvious. Intrigue first.]**

> If your revision feels good, it's probably not working.

*Pause. Look at the camera.*

> Let me explain.

*On-screen text: "if revision feels good, it's probably not working"*

**[Beat: unpack the statement, keeping loops open, revealing slowly.]**

> You read through your notes, it flows, everything makes sense, you close the book feeling on top of it... then you sit the paper and the page might as well be in another language. So what's going on?

> Your brain has two settings: recognising and recalling. Reading your notes only uses the first one. The info's right there in front of you, so your brain just nods along, yep, seen it, seen it. And that nodding feels like knowing. It isn't.

> But the exam only tests the second setting. Blank page, clock running, nothing to look at. And the only way to train that is to practise it, which feels bad. You blank, you get things wrong.

**[Beat: the explanation, landing the quote with its real meaning.]**

> But that struggle isn't it failing, that's the work. In medicine we'd call that the treatment working. It was never a problem with you, it's a problem with your technique, and technique's fixable. So next session, spend less time where it feels smooth and more where it hurts a bit. If your revision feels good... now you know why.

*On-screen text at the end: the quote again, now underneath: "feels good = recognising. feels hard = learning."*

**Caption:** The most useful sentence I know about revision. #alevels #alevelrevision #year13 #studytips

**Why this should hold:** the quote is genuinely ambiguous for the first few seconds, "so what's going on?" opens a loop that only closes at the reveal, and the quote repeating at the end with new meaning is the rewatch trigger. Lands on the "not you, your technique" reframe Curtis and Tom flagged as the best line in the niche. Middle explanation cut by a third so it keeps moving.

## Script 4: Broll and voice over

**Title for your own tracking:** "3 habits, filmed"
**Length target:** 35 to 45 seconds, fully voice over. Record the VO first, then film broll to match it. **Wardrobe:** scrubs for the credibility shots, then normal desk shots.

**[Beat: proven hook]** *(VO over broll: you dropping your bag, sitting at the desk, opening a textbook)*

> You revise for hours and still get the same grade as the mate who crammed the night before. Not because they're smarter. Because hours don't count, sessions that stick do.

*On-screen text: "why the crammer beats you"*

**[Beat: establish credibility]** *(broll: you in scrubs, lanyard visible, making tea after a shift)*

> I'm a doctor, and six years ago I was sat exactly where you are. Since then I've worked with over 1,000 A-level students.

**[Beat: level with the audience, "I know how it feels to..."]** *(broll: closing a textbook, rubbing your eyes, staring at a full page of notes)*

> So I know the feeling of closing the books after a whole evening and realising you can't remember any of it.

**[Beat: introduce the list]** *(broll: clean shot of a blank page and a pen picked up)*

> Three habits that make your hours actually count.

**[Beat: broll of you doing each thing, VO matching what's on screen]**

> One, start with a blank page, not your notes. Two minutes, write what you remember from last time. It warms up your recall and shows you where to start.

*(Broll: hand writing fast on a blank sheet, notes pushed aside, still closed.)*

> Two, end with a closed-book test. Five minutes, everything you just learned, out of your head onto the page. That's the bit that files it away.

*(Broll: textbook closing, phone timer set to 5:00, more writing.)*

> Three, mark yourself in a different colour. The red bits aren't failures, they're your easiest marks to gain, and that's where tomorrow starts.

*(Broll: red pen correcting the page, then a close-up of the corrected page with red gaps filled in.)*

**[Beat: close]** *(broll: the finished page slid into a folder)*

> Save this for your next session.

**Caption:** The 5 minutes at each end of a session matter more than the 3 hours in the middle. #alevels #alevelrevision #year12 #year13 #studytok

**Why this should hold:** the hook adapts the Angry Explainer opener (hours revising vs the mate who crammed) without the insult angle. Every VO line has a literal picture attached, so it works muted and the corrected page is a satisfying payoff. Deliver the "I know the feeling" beat plainly, not sadly.

## Script 5: Method breakdown

**Title for your own tracking:** "The error log breakdown"
**Length target:** 45 to 55 seconds. **Wardrobe:** scrubs, at the desk, a marked past paper and a red pen in front of you to gesture at.
**Note:** deliberately NOT a recall or blurting tip. This is the practice-question half of the system: what you do with a wrong answer after you've marked it. The K, R, W letters are already how Waleed teaches this in the subject email sequence and the diagnostic, so the short matches the site.

**[Beat: proven hook]**

> You've done ten past papers and your grade hasn't moved. It's not the number of papers. It's what you do in the ten minutes after them.

*On-screen text: "10 past papers, same grade"*

**[Beat: hype up the method]**

> This is the highest-yield thing I teach, and it's one page per subject. Your wrong answers are the most valuable material you own, and almost everyone bins them.

**[Beat: introduce what the method is called]**

> It's called an error log.

*On-screen text: "THE ERROR LOG"*

**[Beat: the science explaining the problem, not the method]**

> Here's the problem. You mark the paper, you see the red, you feel a bit rubbish, you move on. Nothing gets fixed, so next paper you drop the exact same marks. Because "I got it wrong" isn't a diagnosis. In hospital, when something goes wrong, we sit down and work out precisely why, so it can't happen twice.

**[Beat: how the method works as the solution]**

> So every dropped mark gets one line, and a letter. K, you didn't know it, that's a content gap. R, you knew it but couldn't get it out under pressure, that's retrieval. W, you knew it and wrote it, just not in the mark scheme's language. Three different problems with three different fixes, and now you know which one you've actually got.

**[Beat: how to implement it]**

> One page per subject. Mark it meanly with the real mark scheme, log every lost mark with its letter and what the scheme actually wanted, then read that log before your next paper. Retest it weekly. That's the bit everyone skips.

**[Beat: close]**

> Follow for the stuff school never taught you.

*On-screen text during the letters: "K: didn't know it" / "R: knew it, couldn't produce it" / "W: knew it, wrong wording". Then for implementation: "1. mark it meanly" / "2. one line + letter per lost mark" / "3. read it before the next paper" / "4. retest weekly".*

**Caption:** Ten past papers without an error log is just the same mistake ten times. #alevels #alevelrevision #year12 #year13 #studytok

**Why this should hold:** the hook is a different felt moment from the rest of the batch (effort going in, grade not moving) and it names a frustration every serious student has had. The method is genuinely new material here: nothing else in the batch covers what to do with a wrong answer, so it pairs with the past-paper habit instead of repeating the recall lesson. The K, R, W letters are the memorable, screenshot-able payload, they are already Waleed's own framing on the site, and the hospital line is credibility only he can use.

## Script 6: Rankings

**Title for your own tracking:** "A* student or D student"
**Length target:** 30 to 40 seconds. Fast. **Wardrobe:** normal clothes. **Extra person needed:** someone off camera reading the statements flat. Cut on each new statement to keep pace.

**Setup note:** react instantly, no thinking pause. The fun is the confidence. Verdicts are one line each, the reference reels barely explain at all; resist adding a second sentence.

**[Beat: someone off camera says a statement. You drop it into a category: A* student or D student.]**

> **Off camera:** I reread my notes the night before the exam.
> **You:** D student. That's recognition, not recall.

> **Off camera:** I start past papers before I've finished the content.
> **You:** A* student. Feels wrong, works brilliantly.

> **Off camera:** I highlight in four different colours.
> **You:** D student. Gorgeous notes, though.

> **Off camera:** I test myself and get loads of it wrong.
> **You:** A* student. Every wrong answer now is a mark you keep in the exam.

> **Off camera:** I spent my whole Sunday making a revision timetable.
> **You:** Oof. That was me. The timetable is not the work.

> **Off camera:** I finish every topic writing it all out from memory.
> **You:** That's blurting. Most A* thing you've said all day.

**[Beat: close]**

> Comment your habit, I'll rank it in the next one.

*On-screen text: "A* STUDENT" in green or "D STUDENT" in red stamps on with each verdict. Keep a persistent two-column scoreboard at the top of frame (A* side, D side) and file a small thumbnail of each habit into its column as you rule, so the tally builds through the video (the device the red flag reel and the 14-second apps reel both use).*

**Caption:** Be honest about number one. Comment a habit and I'll rank it. #alevels #alevelrevision #year12 #year13 #studytok

**Why this should hold:** it's a comment engine. Every verdict invites disagreement, the timetable confession keeps it warm not smug, and the CTA turns the comments into next week's episode. Verdicts cut to one line each to match the reels' machine-gun pace; the building scoreboard gives a reason to stay for the tally. Most repeatable format of the batch.

## Script 7: Caption reel (tracker idea: "How to become neurologically addicted to studying")

**Format from the reference (bella, 127,946 likes):** 6 to 8 seconds of quiet b-roll on loop with one static title. No talking. The caption is the content. Cheapest film of the batch.

**Film:** one slow phone shot of your real desk mid session: open textbook, blurt sheet with two colours, pen down, mug. A scrubs angle with the stethoscope on the desk is a good alternate. No face needed.

*On-screen text (static, centred, serif like the reference): "how to make your brain crave revision (from a doctor)"*

**Caption (this is the actual content):**

> Doctors learn more content in a year than A-level students see in two. Here's how I trained my brain to want the work.
>
> 1. Give your brain a starting cue. Same desk, same drink, same no-lyrics playlist. Soon the cue alone flips you into focus.
>
> 2. Make the first two minutes stupidly easy. Open the page, write one line from memory. Starting is the hard part, so shrink it.
>
> 3. Stop while you've still got something left. End on momentum and your brain files studying as rewarding, not draining.
>
> 4. Keep the score visible. Tick off topics, stack your fixed blurt sheets. Seeing the pile grow is a reward every session.
>
> 5. Leave one question unfinished on purpose. Your brain hates an open loop and keeps chewing on it.
>
> 6. Phone in another room for the first 30 minutes. Revision can't compete with an infinite scroll, so don't make it try.
>
> 7. End every session testing yourself, notes closed. The test is what tells your brain this matters.
>
> 8. Same time every day beats double the hours at random. Your brain starts expecting focus at that hour.
>
> 9. Reward yourself right after, not hours later. Habits form when the reward closely follows the work.
>
> 10. Stop waiting for motivation. Your brain gets attached to what you repeat, not what you feel inspired by.
>
> Follow for the stuff school never taught you.

**Hashtags:** #alevels #alevelrevision #studytips #year12 #year13

**Why this should hold:** the reference proves the format: an intriguing brain-science title over calm study b-roll, caption doing the teaching. The doctor angle is the differentiator none of the reference creators have, and every point is doable tonight, which is what earns the saves this format lives on.

## Script 8: Ratings carousel (tracker idea: "Rate all the study methods I use as…")

**Format from the reference (stharzzzz, 84,700 likes, 34,704 saves):** a photo carousel, not a video. Cover slide with the identity hook, then one slide per method: name, score, blunt one to two line verdict. Study photos as backgrounds.

**Photos:** your own real phone photos, per our platform rules: your desk, a blurt sheet mid fix, a marked past paper, the tracker on screen, a scrubs shot for the cover. No stock images.

**Slides:**

> **Cover:** Rating the study methods I used to get into medicine (and the ones I tell my 1,000+ students to drop)

> **1. Blurt and Fix. 10/10.** Notes closed, write everything, fix the gaps in a second colour. That viral carousel that rated blurting 1/10? They skipped the fix. That's writing, not revising.

> **2. Rereading your notes. 2/10.** Feels calm, does almost nothing. Trains recognising; exams test recall.

> **3. Highlighting. 2/10.** Beautiful textbook, brain never involved. Fine for marking what to blurt later. Useless as the revision itself.

> **4. Flashcards. 8/10.** Done right, they're spaced retrieval, exactly what memory wants. The card has to make you struggle before you flip it.

> **5. Mind maps. 5/10.** Good for seeing how a topic connects the first time. Bad as a memory tool, pretty branches aren't recall.

> **6. Past papers, mark scheme open. 9/10.** Mark your own answer line by line and you learn what the examiner pays for. Only loses a point because most people start too late.

> **Last slide:** The pattern: if it feels smooth, it's training the wrong skill. If it makes you pull things out of your head, it's working. Follow for the stuff school never taught you.

**Caption:** Rated honestly, as someone who marks the papers you're revising for. Which one do you disagree with? #alevels #alevelrevision #studytok #year12 #year13

**Why this should hold:** identity in the cover, disagreement in the scores, and a direct answer to the viral 1/10 blurting claim without naming anyone. Ratings carousels are save magnets (the reference has almost 35,000 saves), and the last slide gives the list one idea to remember. Verdicts trimmed to carousel length.

## Script 9: Red flag, green flag (tracker idea, Tom Vorselen reel)

**Format from the reference (Tom Vorselen, 18,856 likes, 1,849 comments, 1:44):** talking head, persistent RED FLAG / GREEN FLAG scoreboard, one habit per title card, instant verdict plus ONE tight science line, thumbnail files into its column. Films solo. **Length target:** 40 to 50 seconds (the reference runs long at 1:44; this is cut to move). **Wardrobe:** scrubs.

**[Open, straight in, no preamble]**

> Study habits, red flag or green flag. From a doctor. Go.

*On-screen: the two-column scoreboard appears.*

> Music with lyrics while you revise? Red flag. Your brain can't process the words and the work at once.

> Revising in bed? Red flag. Your brain links that spot with sleep, so both get worse.

> Testing yourself before you feel ready? Green flag. The struggle to recall is what wires it in.

> Pulling an all-nighter before the exam? Red flag, and this one's medical. Sleep is when your brain files the day into memory.

> Explaining a topic out loud to nobody? Green flag. If you can't say it simply, you don't know it yet.

> Coming back to a topic three days later, not the same night? Green flag. A bit of forgetting makes it stick.

**[Close, tally on screen]**

> Three red, three green. Comment a habit, I'll flag it in part two.

*On-screen text: each habit gets a title card as you name it; verdict stamps RED FLAG or GREEN FLAG; thumbnail slides into its column. Final frame shows the full scoreboard.*

**Caption:** Doctor rates your study habits. Comment yours for part two. #alevels #alevelrevision #studytok #year12 #year13

**Why this should hold:** it's the reference's exact engine, verdicts plus one science line and a visible scoreboard, but cut to short-form pace (the reference's 1:44 is long; each line here is under 20 words). The part two CTA farms the comments, the way the reference pulled 1,849 of them. Overlaps script 6 in family but differs in tiers, delivery and habits, so they can run weeks apart.

## Script 10: Bad, good, excellent (tracker idea: the 14-second apps reel, adapted to study methods)

**Format from the reference (sofieestudies, 187,837 likes, 14 seconds):** talking head, persistent three-column scoreboard (Bad / Good / Excellent), a category flashes up, three things drop into their slots as you name them. Roughly three words per item. No explanations. This is the fastest, highest-performing shape in the tracker, so keep it BRUTALLY short. **Length target:** 15 to 20 seconds. **Wardrobe:** normal clothes.

**[Open, straight in]**

> Study methods, bad, good, excellent. From a doctor.

*On-screen: three columns appear, Bad / Good / Excellent.*

**[Category one flashes up: "Learning it"]**

> Learning a topic. Rereading, bad. Watching a video, good. Teaching it out loud, excellent.

*(Each drops into its column as you say it.)*

**[Category two: "Testing it"]**

> Testing yourself. Recognising the answer, bad. Flashcards, good. Blurting on a blank page, excellent.

**[Category three: "Timing it"]**

> Timing it. Night-before cram, bad. Spacing it out, good. Past papers under the clock, excellent.

**[Close]**

> Follow for the stuff school never taught you.

*On-screen text: category label per round; each method drops into Bad, Good or Excellent as you name it; final frame shows the full grid.*

**Caption:** Bad, good, excellent: study methods, from a doctor. Disagree with any? #alevels #alevelrevision #studytok #year12 #year13

**Why this should hold:** this is the exact shape of the 187K-like reel, the highest performer in the tracker, and it's built for the pace Waleed asked for: three words an item, the grid does the explaining. It also teaches the whole recognition-vs-recall hierarchy in 15 seconds without a single "because". Films solo, no off-camera voice needed. If one grid pops, the format is infinite: new categories every week.

## Filming order suggestion

Batch in one sitting: 10, 2, 6 and 9 first while energy is high (they're the fast ones), then 1 and 5, then record the script 4 voice over and pick up its broll plus the scrubs shots for 3 last. Script 7's b-roll and script 8's photos take ten minutes at the end while the desk is still messy and real. Record every script twice; the second take is nearly always the looser, better one.
