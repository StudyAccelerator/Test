## 3. How the group grows (the promotion plan)

Ordered by cost and trust, warmest first.

1. **The founding seed (today).** WhatsApp the March cohort families and any parents in live DM threads (drafts in section 5). Fifteen trusted members and three real threads beat two hundred cold ones.
2. **The cohort (Saturday).** Every Summer Accelerator family gets the group in onboarding as the default place where you post everything. These parents already paid; they are the most invested voices the group will ever have.
3. **The email lists (Friday).** One dedicated invite to "Revision Diagnostic Parents" and the parent-guide list (draft in section 6), sent once the room has furniture. Then the standing PS line goes into the parent email sequence (section 6), so every future diagnostic parent hears about the group automatically.
4. **Results week in the big groups (9 to 21 Aug).** Your twenty minutes of daily replies continues as normal, diagnostic-first script for tutor requests. But in results-day threads and panicking-parent threads, where the group's rules allow it, the helpful pointer that week is: "I've pinned a parents' guide to results day in my free group, A-Level Parents UK, you're welcome in." The free group is promotable in places the business never was (section 7 has the exact reply endings).
5. **The profile.** The group joins the diagnostic and parent guide as a pinned profile link, so every profile click from group replies has a warm place to land.
6. **Later, once ads run again:** the group is a second destination to test against the diagnostic, and the entry-question archive is the ad copy bank. Nothing to do this week.

The growth loop to protect: replies in big groups make parents click your profile; the
profile shows the group; the group shows a doctor answering every question within a
day; the entry questions feed the email list; the email list fills the programmes. The
group only compounds if the reply-to-every-comment habit holds, so the daily 30 minutes
is the actual growth engine.

## 4. The posts

All written to the Facebook voice rules: typed-on-a-phone, question or situation first,
credentials in passing brackets, 50 to 130 words, no images unless a real phone photo
earns its place, end on a question a parent can answer from the kitchen table. Links
live in comments, never the post body. Reply to every comment inside an hour where
humanly possible; the thread is the content.

### Post 1: the pinned welcome (post first, pin it)

> Welcome in. I'm Waleed, an NHS doctor, and outside the hospital I help A-level students learn to revise properly. I run A-Level Accelerators, so full transparency: I sell programmes, I'll only ever mention them in clearly marked posts, and everything in this group is free either way.
>
> This is the room where A-level parents get straight answers. Revision that actually works, results day, UCAS, predicted grades, and what to do when the effort is going in and the grades aren't coming out.
>
> House rules are short: no advertising (tutors and companies get removed, no warnings), never share someone else's child's details, and be kind. Everyone here is doing their best with a stressed teenager.
>
> So I know what to write about: what year is your child going into this September, and what's the current worry? I read everything.

**First comment:** If you want somewhere to start, the free Revision Diagnostic is the
tool I'd run first. 20 questions about how your child revises, instant report on where
the marks are leaking: https://alevelaccelerators.com/revision-diagnostic/?for=parents

### Post 2: furniture, the normalisation ask (same day, a few hours later)

> Honest question for the parents of teenagers doing A-levels: when you ask how revision's going, what do you actually get back?
>
> I ask because "fine" is the answer I hear about most from parents, and it usually means one of two things. Either it genuinely is fine, or the door closes and "fine" is doing a lot of heavy lifting.
>
> I spend a lot of time with A-level students (I'm an NHS doctor the rest of the week), and the ones saying "fine" to their parents are often the ones quietly drowning in it. Not because they're lazy. Because they don't have the words for what's not working.
>
> So, genuinely: what's the answer in your house? "Fine", full sentences, or somewhere in between?

### Post 3: furniture, teach one thing (day two, morning)

> One thing I wish every A-level parent knew: re-reading notes feels like revising and does almost nothing. The page looks familiar, so the brain files it as known. Exams don't test familiar. They test whether it comes back with the book shut.
>
> The kitchen-table check takes two minutes. Ask your child to explain one topic to you from memory, like you know nothing about it. If it comes out in their own words, it's learned. If they need the notes open, that topic was recognition, not knowledge, and it would've leaked marks in the exam.
>
> (Recall under pressure is most of my day job as a doctor. It's also exactly what exams mark.)
>
> Try it tonight and tell me how it went. I'll tell you what the result means.

### Post 4: furniture, the confession (day two, evening)

> Something I tell every parent eventually, so I'll say it here early: I got the grades that got me into medicine with a revision method that was honestly terrible. I worked brutal hours and succeeded on volume, not technique, because nobody ever taught me how to revise. School teaches what to learn. Nobody teaches how.
>
> Six years on I've worked with over 1,000 A-level students and it's the same story in most houses. The "lazy" teenager usually isn't lazy. They're untaught, and they're tired, and those look identical from the kitchen doorway.
>
> If your child works hard and the marks aren't moving, tell me the subject below. I'll tell you where those marks usually go missing.

### Post 5: results week, how the morning actually works (Sunday 9 Aug)

> Results day is Thursday. Here's how the morning actually works, because knowing the mechanics lowers everyone's heart rate.
>
> Results are released to students from 8am, at school or in the exam board portals. UCAS gets the grades directly, so your child's UCAS Hub updates that morning with the decision: firm choice confirmed, insurance confirmed, or neither. The Hub often says it before anyone's opened an envelope.
>
> Two things worth doing before Thursday: make sure your child can log into UCAS Hub (password resets on the day are misery), and agree who's going with them or whether they'd rather check alone. Both are fine. It's their morning.
>
> What's the plan in your house for Thursday, and what's your child hoping to see?

### Post 6: results week, Clearing without the panic (Monday 10 Aug)

> Clearing has a scary reputation and mostly shouldn't. It's how tens of thousands of students find a course every year, including plenty who missed by one grade and plenty who did better than expected and traded up.
>
> The mechanics: if the Hub says neither choice confirmed, Clearing choices can be added from 1pm on results day. The morning is for phone calls to universities, and the offers made on those calls go to the student who's ready.
>
> Worth prepping Wednesday night, just in case: UCAS personal ID written down, phone charged, a quiet room, and a shortlist of three or four backup courses with their Clearing numbers. Ninety minutes of prep nobody may ever need, and if they do need it, it's gold.
>
> Has anyone here been through Clearing with an older child? What do you wish you'd known?

### Post 7: results week, appeals and remarks (Tuesday 11 Aug)

> While we're ahead of Thursday: if a grade comes back that doesn't look right, there's a proper route, and it has a deadline.
>
> It's called a review of marking, it goes through the school (they submit it, not you), and for anyone with a university place hanging on the result there's a priority deadline of 20 August. That's one week after results day, which is why it pays to know the route before you need it.
>
> Two honest cautions from someone who's watched this play out: marks can go down as well as up in a review, and the first conversation should always be with the exams officer at school, who's done this a hundred times.
>
> Hopefully none of us needs this post. Bookmark it anyway. Any questions about how it works, ask below and I'll answer everything.

### Post 8: results week, the breakfast script (Wednesday 12 Aug)

> Tomorrow morning, before the grades exist, might be the most useful five minutes of the whole week.
>
> Part of my job as a doctor is difficult conversations, and the rule that holds everywhere: decide what you'll say before the emotion arrives, not during. So tonight or at breakfast, one sentence: "Whatever's in that envelope tomorrow, we'll sort the next step together, and nothing in it changes what I think of you."
>
> That's it. Not "you'll smash it" (pressure dressed as support), not "grades aren't everything" (they'll hear it as pre-emptive consolation). Just: the result is information, the plan comes after, and home is safe either way.
>
> Teenagers act like they don't hear these things. They hear everything.
>
> How's everyone feeling about tomorrow? One word answers welcome.

### Post 9: THE results day thread (Thursday 13 Aug, posted before 8am, pin it for the day)

> It's results day. I'm keeping this thread open all day.
>
> Whatever the morning brings: the grades they wanted, a near miss, or a proper shock, post it here (no school names, no other people's children) and I'll tell you the honest next move. Confirmation, insurance, Clearing from 1pm, reviews of marking, or just what to say at lunch. No question is too small and nothing is silly today of all days.
>
> And if the news is good, post that too. This group should see what the good mornings look like.
>
> Deep breaths. It's information, not a verdict. I'm here all day.

**Rota honesty check before posting:** only promise "all day" if Thursday is genuinely
clear. If you're working, the honest version wins: swap the first line for "I'm checking
this thread at every break today, and everything gets an answer by this evening."

### Post 10: results day evening check-in (Thursday 13 Aug, ~7pm)

> Checking in now the dust is settling. However today went in your house: if the place is confirmed, congratulations, go celebrate and mute the group for a week, you've earned it.
>
> If it's not sorted yet, you're exactly who this group is for tonight. Clearing lines stay open for days (the good courses don't all vanish by teatime, whatever the panic says), reviews of marking have until the 20th for priority cases, and tonight the only job is dinner and a pause. The plan looks clearer on Friday morning, every single year.
>
> Post where things stand below if you want a second pair of eyes on the next step. I'll answer everything tonight and tomorrow.

### Post 11: the day after (Friday 14 Aug)

> The day after results day sorts every house into one of three rooms, so here's the next move for each.
>
> Place confirmed: done. The only useful thing now is a proper rest, then September prep in a few weeks.
>
> Near miss, holding an offer through Clearing or insurance: let them sit with it for 48 hours before any big second-guessing. A course chosen calmly on Sunday beats one grabbed in Friday's adrenaline.
>
> Grades way off and no place: this is fixable, and quickly. Clearing runs for weeks, reviews of marking close for priority cases on the 20th, and for some students a retake year is genuinely the strong move, not the sad one.
>
> Which room is your house in? Say the word and I'll map the next step with you.

### September, week one (the locked rhythm starts Monday 1 Sep)

**Monday conversation question:**

> First week of September. Year 12 parents: how's the leap landing? The jump from GCSE to A-level is the steepest of the lot (bigger than A-level to my medical degree, honestly), and most students hit October before they admit their GCSE revision method stopped working.
>
> Year 13 parents: predicted grades get set from the first tests of this term, and they're what goes on the UCAS form. September work moves them more than any other month's.
>
> So, either year: what's the September mood in your house? Fired up, coasting, or pretending it's still August?

**Wednesday method post:**

> The single revision upgrade I'd give every A-level student for September: the blank page test, once a week per subject. Shut the book, write everything they know about one topic from memory, then open the book and mark the gaps in a different colour.
>
> The gaps are the revision list. Not the whole textbook, the gaps. That's the difference between revising what's comfortable and revising what's missing, and it's most of the difference between a B and an A (the marks live in the second colour).
>
> Ten minutes, one topic, tonight, any subject. If your child tries it, tell me what the page looked like. Mostly full? Mostly gaps? Both mean something useful.

**Friday AMA:**

> Weekend ask-me-anything. Drop any A-level question below between now and Sunday: revision methods, predicted grades, UCAS, a subject that's wobbling, why the bedroom light is on at 1am. Nothing's too small or too specific (no school names or other people's kids, as ever).
>
> I'll answer everything by Sunday evening. Ask away.

## 7. Promoting the group in other people's groups (results week, rules permitting)

The standing rules hold: tutor requests still get the diagnostic-first Lucy Albert
script, never a group plug. The group gets mentioned only where it genuinely answers
the thread, and only in groups whose rules allow it. Two endings that work:

- On a panicking results-week thread, after a real answer: "I've pinned a full parents' guide to results day in my free group, A-Level Parents UK, if it helps to have it all in one place. You're welcome in."
- On a "how do I help them revise" thread, after a real answer: "I run a free group for A-level parents (A-Level Parents UK) where I answer exactly this every day, if you want more than one comment's worth."

Never both audiences, never in a group that bans group shares, never twice in the same
group in a week, and the answer above the plug has to stand on its own.
