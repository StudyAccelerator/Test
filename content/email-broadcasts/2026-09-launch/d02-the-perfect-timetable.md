Confession time. When I was in Year 12, I once spent six hours in one sitting making the perfect revision timetable.

I mean perfect. Colour-coded by subject. Every hour accounted for. Breaks placed like a professional had done it. I sat back and looked at it and felt like I'd already revised. Honestly, making it felt more productive than any actual revision I'd done that month.

The next week, I didn't touch it again.

Not once. And it wasn't laziness. The plan just asked too much of me. Every evening I had to open it, work out where I was, decide whether to follow it or catch up, feel guilty about yesterday's missed session, renegotiate the whole thing. By day four, opening the timetable felt worse than not opening it, so I stopped.

I promise this is a judge-free zone, because I've since watched hundreds of students do exactly the same thing. The pattern is always the same: the plan dies at the moment it asks you to make decisions. What should I do tonight? Am I behind? Does the plan still count? Every one of those questions is a chance to give up.

Here's what I eventually worked out, years later, the hard way. The students who revise consistently don't have better timetables and they don't have more willpower. They have fewer decisions. The session is already decided, the topic is already decided, and when a week goes wrong, the plan rebuilds around reality instead of collapsing.
