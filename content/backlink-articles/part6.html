What Should You Actually Pay an A-Level Tutor? The 2026 Price Guide Nobody Publishes

Target keywords: a level tutor cost per hour, how much is a tutor uk, a level tutor prices uk, private tutor cost a level

I get asked about tutoring prices constantly, and the question behind the question is always the same: am I about to waste money? So here is the guide I wish every family got before they opened their wallet, written by someone who teaches for a living and has no interest in dressing the market up.

The going rates, without the fog

For one-to-one A-level tutoring in the UK, £50 an hour is a sensible working average. The marketplaces list tutors from about £20 at the bottom to £70 and beyond at the top, and where a tutor sits in that range mostly tracks three things: qualifications, subject and season. Maths and the sciences run more expensive than the humanities because the demand outstrips the tutors. Prices harden every spring as exams get close and the good tutors fill their diaries. And agencies sit a layer of commission between you and the person doing the teaching, which is worth knowing when you compare a £45 platform rate against a £45 independent one.

Stretch those hourly numbers across a school year and the picture sharpens. A weekly hour at the average rate is roughly £200 a month for one subject. Run that from September to exams and you have spent north of £1,500, per subject. Two weak subjects doubles it. That is the real price of the default option, and most families never do this multiplication until the invoices have already arrived.

The alternative maths

Live small-group teaching splits a specialist's hour across the group, which drops the per-family price to £10 to £15 per teaching hour for the same weekly rhythm. The trade is obvious when you say it plainly: you give up having the hour steered around one student, and you gain two to three times the expert hours for the same budget. Which side of that trade is right depends entirely on the problem. A student with one collapsed topic, or one who will not open their mouth in any group, gets real value from one-to-one. A student who is working hard and underperforming across a whole subject usually needs volume of expert exam practice, and volume is exactly what group pricing buys.

Price is the wrong dial to stare at

Here is the uncomfortable bit for my own industry: the hourly rate predicts very little about results. What predicts results is what fills the hour. Sessions that live inside past-paper questions and mark schemes move grades. Sessions that re-explain the textbook feel wonderful and move almost nothing, because understanding content was rarely the bottleneck in the first place. The exam is a performance under time pressure against a mark scheme, and performance improves by rehearsing it.

So before you pay anyone, at any price, ask one question: what proportion of a typical session is spent working exam questions? Then ask how they identify why marks are being lost before they start teaching. A tutor with good answers to both is worth their rate, whatever it is. A tutor without them is expensive at any price, because the meter is running while the wrong problem gets solved.

The honest bottom line

Budget around £50 an hour for competent one-to-one and £1,500 plus per subject across a year of it. Choose it deliberately for narrow, specific problems. For broad underperformance, structured group teaching at a fraction of the hourly cost usually buys more progress per pound, provided it is built on exam questions rather than lectures. And whichever route you take, judge it in marks, not vibes: if six weeks of any tutoring has not changed what happens when your child sits a past paper, change the tutoring.
