{$name},

This Thursday, 8am, results. If that's you, this Session is yours start to finish. If it's not you this year, read it anyway. Your turn at this morning comes round eventually, and it's a lot easier when you already know how it works.

One sentence to hold onto first: Thursday is a sorting morning, not a judgement morning. Whatever the slip says, it sorts you onto a route, and every route has a next move. Calm beats clever on Thursday, and calm comes from knowing the moves in advance. So here they are.

**Wednesday night, ten minutes**

Sort the practical stuff: what time school opens, and whether you're going in. Go in if you possibly can. If it looks rough, teachers fight hardest for the student standing in front of them.

Write three numbers on actual paper: your firm choice's admissions line, your insurance's, and UCAS (0371 468 0468). Paper, because your phone will pick Thursday morning to die on you.

Then charge the phone anyway, and sleep. The result was set in June. Nothing you feel tonight changes it, so spend the evening on something that isn't this.

**The morning, in order**

The UCAS Hub updates from around 8am and tells you whether your place is confirmed. It won't show the grades themselves; those are on the slip at school.

**Met your firm offer:** confirmed automatically. Nothing to do except thank whoever kept you fed this year. Go celebrate properly.

**Missed firm, met insurance:** your insurance confirms automatically. It's real, and you chose it for a reason. If you're gutted, the options below still exist, but sit with it a day before trading a confirmed place for a maybe.

**Just missed, by one grade or so:** phone your firm before you accept anything else. The script: your name, UCAS ID, course, then "I've missed my offer by one grade and I'd like to ask whether you can still take me." Universities take near-misses more often than students believe, but only from the ones who call. If it's a no, ask about the same course with a foundation year, then move to clearing.

**Missed both:** clearing, and clearing is normal, not a punishment. Tens of thousands of students find a place through it every year, and good courses put up spaces on Thursday morning. Same script: name, UCAS ID, grades, "do you have places on [course]?" Have your personal statement open, some will ask about it. And you make the calls, not your parents. Universities will only speak to the applicant.

**Beat your offer:** you can look upward through clearing too, if you want to. Only trade up for a course you actually want more, never just because it sits higher up a league table.

**Grades feel wrong:** talk to your teachers on the day about a review of marking. The school submits it, and marks can move down as well as up, so it's a decision for papers that were genuinely close to a boundary, not something to do automatically. If a university place depends on it, say so; the priority deadlines are tight.

**The afternoon**

Whatever happened, do something kind for yourself that has nothing to do with school. It's an emotional day even when it goes well. And if it didn't go well: one morning has put you on a longer route. It hasn't measured your worth, and it hasn't closed the door on the life you wanted. The longer route still gets you to the same place. I've watched it happen too many times to count.

**The clinic**

"My grades are going to be bad. Should I even go in?"

Yes. Go in. Bad news feels far worse when you're sitting at home with it on your own. At school, everyone around you is having the same morning, and that honestly makes it easier to carry. It's also the smartest move you can make: the students who turn up get their teachers on the phones for them within the hour. And you don't have to pretend to be fine. Get your slip, get your teachers helping, and leave whenever you like.

**High-yield / low-yield**

High-yield: the three phone numbers on paper, Wednesday night.

Low-yield: refreshing the UCAS Hub at 6am. It updates when it updates. If there's an important phone call to make at 9, a proper night's sleep will do more for it than an early alarm.
