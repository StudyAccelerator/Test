Right. Session one. The idea everything else in these Sessions is built on.

When a student tells me "I'm doing everything and the grade won't move", I ask one question: which tier are you training? Almost nobody can answer, because nobody's ever shown them the tiers. Every mark you'll ever earn comes from one of four tiers:

Tier 1: Knowledge. You understand the content when it's in front of you.

Tier 2: Recall. You can produce it from memory. Notes closed, nothing prompting you.

Tier 3: Application. You can use it on a question you've never seen before.

Tier 4: Exam mastery. You can do all of that on the day, to time, under pressure, using the words the mark scheme gives marks for.

School lives almost entirely on Tier 1. Lessons explain content. Homework checks content. And "revision", the way most students do it (re-reading, highlighting, making the notes look nice), is just more Tier 1. Then the exam comes along and gives nearly all of its marks for Tiers 2, 3 and 4.

I fell straight into this myself. My A-level revision was re-reading my notes until they felt familiar, and familiar felt like knowing. It wasn't. The exam hall feeling of "I knew this yesterday, where's it gone" isn't a memory problem. It's a student who trained Tier 1 for two years and got tested on Tier 4.

Here's the useful part. The tiers stack on top of each other, so your grade is set by your first weak tier, not your strongest one. Working below your weak tier feels comfortable, but it earns you almost nothing. Working at your weak tier feels awkward. That awkward feeling is how you know you're doing the work that actually moves your grade.

Tonight, run the ten minute audit:

1. Take one subject. Ask, honestly, tier by tier: could I explain this topic with the book open? Could I write it out with the book closed? Could I answer a question on it I've never seen before? Could I do all of that against the clock?

2. Score each tier out of 5. The first tier that scores under 4 is your weak tier.

3. That weak tier is where your revision should live now. Not the tiers below it. You already own those.

Ten minutes per subject, and you'll know more about your own revision than most students find out in two years.

**The clinic**

A question I keep getting from students starting Year 13 in September: "Should I get ahead on next year's content over summer, or go back over Year 12?"

Audit first, then both, in that order. Year 13 content is built straight on top of Year 12, so a weak topic doesn't stay one weak topic: next year's work lands on top of it and the gap grows. Fix your three weakest Year 12 topics per subject first, and fix them at Tier 2, not Tier 1: book closed, from memory. Then preview September's first topics. Do both and you walk into September ahead twice over.
