Welcome to this week's Sunday Session, the email where I share one thing school rarely teaches, that you can pass on at home straight away.

Your child is probably a couple of weeks into the new school year now, so the first proper tests are just around the corner. And in Year 13, those early tests are a big part of the evidence teachers use to set predicted grades, so every mark in them really does count.

That's why this week is about the most frustrating marks to lose: the ones your child already knows, but never gets the time to write down.

I see it all the time. A student knows the content, sits the paper, and the last 6 mark question is still blank when time runs out. It almost never happens because they're slow. It happens because nobody ever showed them how to budget their time in an exam.

As a doctor, I think about this a lot like triage in A&E. When the department is busy, you don't spend forty minutes on a sprained ankle while someone with chest pain is waiting. You decide where your time is worth the most and you stick to it. Exams work exactly the same way, and here's the simple version to share with your child.

**Step 1: Work out the number.** As soon as the exam starts, divide the total minutes by the total marks. A 2 hour paper worth 100 marks gives 1.2 minutes per mark, and for most A-level papers it comes out at a little over a minute.

**Step 2: Give every question a budget.** Multiply its marks by that number, so a 6 mark question gets about 7 minutes. Then halfway through the time, they want to be about halfway through the marks, so they know straight away if they're falling behind.

**Step 3: Use the move on rule.** If they've spent double a question's budget and they're still stuck, they write whatever they can, put a star next to it and come back at the end. Leaving a 2 marker half done to go and get a 6 marker isn't giving up, it's just good maths!

**How to bring it up without it turning into a row:** next time your child mentions a test, instead of asking "have you revised?", try asking "what's your number for that paper?" It's a question they can actually answer, it shows you're interested in how they're working, and it opens up a much better conversation. If they look at you blankly, forwarding them this email is a very easy way in.
