Welcome to this week's Sunday Session. It's the email where I share one thing school rarely teaches, that you can pass on to your child straight away.

Your child is probably a couple of weeks into the new school year now. That means their first proper tests are coming soon.

In Year 13, these early tests matter even more. Teachers use them as evidence when they set predicted grades, so every mark counts.

So this week is about the most frustrating marks to lose: the ones your child already knows, but runs out of time to write down.

I see it all the time. A student knows the content, sits the paper, and the last 6 mark question is still blank when time runs out.

This almost never happens because they're slow. It happens because nobody ever showed them how to plan their time in an exam.

As a doctor, I think about this a lot like triage in A&E.

When it's busy, we don't spend forty minutes on a sprained ankle while someone with chest pain is waiting. We decide where our time matters most, and we stick to that plan.

Exams work in exactly the same way. Here are three simple steps to share with your child.

**Step 1: Work out the time per mark.**

When the exam starts, divide the total minutes by the total marks. For example, a 2 hour paper worth 100 marks gives 1.2 minutes per mark.

For most A-level papers, it's a little over a minute per mark.

**Step 2: Give every question a time budget.**

Multiply the marks by the time per mark. So a 6 mark question gets about 7 minutes.

Halfway through the exam, they should be about halfway through the marks. If they're behind, they know straight away.

**Step 3: Use the move on rule.**

If they've spent double the time on a question and they're still stuck, they write what they can, put a star next to it, and come back at the end.

Leaving a 2 mark question half done to go and get a 6 mark question isn't giving up, it's just good maths!

**A better question to ask at home**

Next time your child mentions a test, try not to ask "Have you revised?"

Instead, ask them: "What's your time per mark for that paper?"

It's a question they can actually answer. It shows you care about how they work, not just how long they work, and it usually leads to a much better conversation. If they're not sure, you can forward them this email.

Timing is only one reason students lose marks they already know. For some, it's exam technique. For others, it's the way they revise, or a few topics they never fully understood.
