# Top 1% Mentorship: the spine

*Written 10 September 2026 for the first two mentorship students (one Year 12, one resitting). This is the document the weekly runsheets hang off. Read it once; after that you only need the runsheet for the week.*

## What this programme is, in one paragraph

A student pays £300 a month for you to fix how they study, never what they study. You diagnose what is broken in their current method, rebuild it in the order that the four tiers demand (knowledge, recall, application, exam mastery), and then train the exam as its own skill until the papers say A*. Every week has one teach, one number that should move, and one thing the student can point at and say "that changed". The subject content is theirs and their school's. Your job is the system that turns content into marks.

## The five rules the plan is built on

1. **Diagnose before you treat, every single week.** The first ten minutes of every session are the scorecard, not the teach. If the number did not move, the session is about why, and the teach waits. A week where you teach the next thing while last week's thing is not working is a wasted week and a wasted £75.
2. **Never the syllabus.** You do not need to know their spec. Every exercise in this plan works on whatever topic the student brings: blurt it, rate it, question it, mark it, time it. If a session drifts into "explain photosynthesis to me", stop and send them to the textbook, the teacher or a Subject Accelerator. That boundary is what makes the programme deliverable by you at scale.
3. **Retrieval first, then spacing, then targeting, then exam craft.** That is the order the diagnostic's bottleneck logic uses and it is the order the runsheets follow. Exam craft on a student who cannot recall is polishing an empty page. Spacing before they have a retrieval method is scheduling re-reading. The order is not negotiable; the pace is.
4. **The number is the retention.** A student stays because they can see the line going up. So the scorecard is the product as much as the calls are. It is filled in by the student before each session, reviewed together, and turned into a one-page before-and-after at every monthly review. Parents get the same page in an email.
5. **Every week ends with one sentence from them.** "What changed this week?" They say it, you write it on the scorecard. Twenty-six of those in a row is the story of the programme, and it is the thing that makes month five feel earned rather than owed.

## The four tiers, mapped onto six months

| Tier | What it is | Months | What the student can do at the end of it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Knowledge | Understanding the content (school's job) | Always, but you never teach it | Route their hours to the topics where the marks are, not the comfortable ones |
| 2. Recall | Producing it from memory, cold | 1 to 2 | Blurt any topic on a blank page, find the gaps, fix only the gaps, and come back on schedule so it stays |
| 3. Application | Using it on unfamiliar questions | 3 to 4 | Work mixed, cold questions; keep an error log; retest until the error is gone |
| 4. Exam mastery | Timing, command words, mark scheme language, calm | 4 to 6 | Sit a full paper at 90 percent of the time, mark it like an examiner, and turn every lost mark into a rewritten answer |

The five systems the diagnostic scores (Method, Retention, Consistency, Targeting, Exam Craft) are how you talk about it to the student. The four tiers are how you talk about it to the parent. Same model, two vocabularies.

## The six phases

| Phase | Sessions | Dates | The question the phase answers | The proof at the end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Diagnose and install | 1 to 4 | 14 Sep to 11 Oct | Where do the hours leak, and what is the retrieval method? | Hours audit before and after; first blurt sheets; baseline timed section |
| 2. Make it stick | 5 to 8 | 12 Oct to 8 Nov | Why does it fade, and why do sessions not start? | Re-blurt gaps shrinking; sessions started on time; second paper vs baseline |
| 3. Aim it | 9 to 12 | 9 Nov to 6 Dec | Where are the marks actually missing? | Avoid-list topics opened; error log retest rate; December test or mock result |
| 4. Train the exam | 13 to 16 | 7 Dec to 17 Jan | How do marks get lost when the knowledge is there? | Timing on target; rewritten answers in scheme wording; January re-rate after the break |
| 5. Pressure and pace | 17 to 20 | 18 Jan to 14 Feb | Can they do it under the clock, on a bad day? | Full papers at 90 percent time; panic errors down; paper grade equivalent climbing |
| 6. Run it without me | 21 to 26 | 15 Feb to 28 Mar | Can they diagnose and plan themselves? | The student runs a session unaided; the ten-week run-in plan is built; the six-month before-and-after page |
| 7. The run-in (months 7 to 9) | 27 to 36 | 12 Apr to exams | Can the system hold at exam intensity? | The exams |

Phase 7 is sketched at the end of the runsheets, not written in full. Write it properly in February, once you have watched two real students through phases 1 to 4. The reason it exists in the plan at all: a student who has done six months of this is six weeks from their exams at the end of March. Nobody sane leaves then. That is the honest answer to "why would they still be paying me in month five": because month five is when the papers start moving fastest, and months seven to nine are the exams themselves.

## The two tracks

Both students run the same spine. The runsheets carry a **Year 12 note** and a **Resit note** wherever the two need different handling. The differences that matter:

**Year 12.** Content is arriving weekly, so the system has to keep up as well as catch up. Their past papers are limited early on, so weeks 1 to 8 use topic questions and AS-style sections rather than full papers, and full papers arrive in phase 5. Their checkpoints are end-of-topic tests, the December assessments, any January mocks, and above all the end-of-Year-12 exams in May or June 2027, which set the predicted grades that go on the UCAS form. That is the money argument for staying past month six: predicted grades are decided in month nine.

**Resit.** The content has been taught once and has faded, and something went wrong last time that nobody has diagnosed. They can sit whole past papers from week 2, so their baseline is a real paper, their error log starts in week 1, and exam craft comes forward by about four weeks (the runsheets say where). They usually have no school structure, so consistency is the system most at risk and the week plan has to be built around work, college or nothing at all. Two admin facts to pin down in session 1: which exam centre they are sitting with, and that centre's entry deadline (the JCQ deadline for the summer series is 21 February; centres set their own earlier dates). Put both on the scorecard.

## The scoreboard

One scorecard per student, filled in by the student before every session (the template is in file 03). It has three layers:

**Weekly (the student's number).**
- Hours: planned vs done, and the split between high-yield (blurt, recall, review, papers) and low-yield (reading, rewriting, watching). Week 1 measures the leak; every later week measures whether it closed.
- Topic ratings: how many topics are Struggling, Shaky and Solid on the tracker's audit. The Struggling count going down is the clearest single line on the chart.
- Retrieval: blurts done, re-blurt gap counts (first blurt vs re-blurt on the same topic), recall sessions done.
- Papers: every timed section or full paper, with the raw score, the percentage, the time used against time allowed, and from phase 4 the grade equivalent from the real boundaries of that paper (the student looks them up on the board's site; never estimate).
- Error log: entries added, entries retested, entries cleared.
- Sessions started on time (a yes/no per planned session, from phase 2).
- The "what changed" line, in their words.

**Monthly (the review page).** One page, built from the weekly rows: the four charts (hours split, Struggling count, paper percentage, error log cleared), the school evidence that landed this month (test scores, teacher comments, a mock grade), three sentences on what changed, and the one thing next month adds. This page is the parent email's attachment and the student's proof.

**Termly (the grade evidence).** School tests, mocks, predicted grades and, for the resit student, full papers under conditions. These are the numbers that move slowest and matter most, and the reason the weekly numbers exist is so that nobody has to wait for them to know whether it is working.

Never invent a grade equivalent. If the paper has no published boundaries, record the percentage and say so.

## The session, minute by minute

Sixty minutes, on Zoom, recorded to the cloud. Same shape every week so the student always knows what is coming and you never need to prepare more than the runsheet.

| Minutes | Block | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 10 | The scorecard | They talk you through it. What moved, what did not, and why. You listen for the real reason, not the reported one. |
| 10 to 40 | The teach and the rebuild | The week's teach, done on their material, live. You do the thing with them, not to them: they blurt on screen, they mark the question, they rate the topic. |
| 40 to 55 | Build next week | The between-session work, placed in their real week with the tracker. Specific days, specific topics, specific sessions. Nothing vague survives this block. |
| 55 to 60 | The one line | "What changed this week?" They say it, you type it into the scorecard, and you name what next week adds. |

If the scorecard shows the week did not happen, the teach waits. Minutes 10 to 40 become the consistency conversation (session 6's material, pulled forward), because there is no point installing spacing on a student who did not open a book.

## What "measurable" means each week

Every runsheet names the number that should move. The rule for keeping a week in the plan was: if you cannot name the number, the week goes. A few of the numbers are small and early (blurt gap counts in week 2) and a few are the only ones that matter (paper grade equivalents in month five). The small ones exist so the student can see movement before the school can.

## Retention, by design

The places students quietly stop seeing the point, and what the plan does about each:

- **Week 3 to 4: the method is uncomfortable.** Blurting is humbling. The plan expects it: session 2 warns them it will feel bad, session 3 shows them the gap count as progress, session 4's review puts the hours leak before-and-after in front of them.
- **Month 3: the dip.** Novelty gone, habits not yet automatic, school grades not yet moved because school tests lag. The plan puts the December test or mock inside month 3, so there is a real result to debrief, and session 12 names the dip out loud before it happens.
- **Christmas: the break resets everything.** Two weeks off with a keep-warm plan (three short sessions a week, no new content), and session 15 opens with a re-rate that shows them exactly what the spacing protected.
- **Month 5: "I know all this now".** Correct, and that is when exam craft starts compounding. Phase 5 is the fastest-moving part of the scorecard because the habits are installed and the marks are all in tier 4. Show them the paper line.
- **Month 6: independence feels like the end.** It is the beginning of the run-in. Session 24's review ends with the ten-week plan to their exams, and the decision to continue is made with the exams on the calendar in front of them.

And the mechanical ones: sessions are booked four weeks ahead around your rota, a missed session becomes a recorded video review of their scorecard within 48 hours rather than a silent gap, and the parent gets the monthly page whether the month was good or bad. A parent who hears from you only when things go well learns to dread your emails.

## What this plan never does

- Teach subject content, mark for content accuracy, or explain a topic.
- Promise a grade. The bar is that a student who does the work ends up with A stars, and the scorecard is how both of you will know. What you say to parents is the approved line: on average, our students jump two grades.
- Depend on you remembering anything between sessions. The scorecard and the session notes carry the memory, so that a second coach could pick a student up in month four from the notes alone (that is the point of file 04).

## How to use the runsheets

Open file 02 at the week. Each runsheet has the same seven headings: why this week, the number that should move, the sixty minutes, what they walk away with, between-session work, deviate if, and the Year 12 and Resit notes. You need nothing else in front of you except their scorecard and the tracker.

Deviating is expected. The "deviate if" line on each runsheet tells you the signals that mean the week should move earlier, later or be swapped. When you deviate, write one line in the session notes saying what you swapped and why, so the pattern across students becomes visible by month three.
