# The teaching curriculum: what you actually teach in sessions 2 to 17

*Written 21 September 2026, after Waleed had run session 1 with his first students and asked the real question: what do I teach for the next sixteen weeks? This file is the teaching content. File 02 (the runsheets) is the session structure. Where the two differ, this file wins, because it is built on the session 1 he actually delivers.*

## What changed, and why this file exists

Waleed's session 1 is better than the one written in the runsheets. He teaches blurting, then runs the **four tier mark loss analysis** on a real past paper: every mark the student dropped gets attributed to Knowledge (never knew it), Recall (knew it, could not retrieve it), Application (knew it, could not use it on this question) or Exam technique (knew it and could use it, lost the mark on wording, timing, command word or a misread).

That produces a percentage split. It is a proper diagnostic instrument, it is his own IP, and every session after it should be the treatment for whichever tier is leaking. The runsheets assumed a softer diagnosis (hours audit plus a live blurt), so their teaching order was generic. This file replaces the teaching content of runsheets 2 to 17. The session shape, the scorecard, the parent updates and the deviate-if logic in file 02 all still stand.

## The retention answer, before the curriculum

The question underneath "what do I teach" is "how do I keep them past four months". Four things hold a student, in order of how much they actually matter.

**1. The four tier split, re-run every four weeks, on a chart.** This is the product. Not the teaching, the measurement. A student who can see Exam technique fall from 34 percent of their lost marks to 11 percent over eight weeks does not cancel, because the line on the chart is theirs and it is moving. Run it in session 1, then sessions 5, 9, 13 and 17, always on a fresh paper of similar difficulty, always with the same four buckets. Put the four bars side by side each time. That single chart is the strongest retention asset in the whole programme, and it costs you fifteen minutes a month.

**2. Assets that compound and only exist inside the programme.** By month four a student should own a spec question bank, an error log with a retest ladder, a first move library, a phrase bank and a self marking calibration record. None of it transfers to a tutor who teaches content. Every month makes those assets more valuable, which is the honest version of a switching cost.

**3. Never repeating a teach.** Sixteen named techniques below, then a reserve bank of twelve more. That is over six months of material before anything repeats, and the repeats that do come are deliberate second passes at a higher standard.

**4. The calendar changing the programme's shape.** October is building, December is the mock run-in, January is the mock autopsy, March is the A star gap, April is the exam protocol. The same student experiences five different programmes in a year without you writing anything new.

And the honest limit: **a Year 13 leaves in June whatever you do.** The year plus retention story is Year 12 students, who run September to September and then again into Year 13. If you want twelve month tenure, recruit Year 12s deliberately from January.

## How the sixteen are ordered

Two rules produced the order, and both matter.

**Order by their split, not by the list.** After session 1 you know which tier holds most of their lost marks. Take the block that owns it first. The default order below assumes a typical split (Recall and Exam technique dominant, which is what most B grade students show), so reorder freely. The sessions are modular by design.

**Alternate slow and fast.** Foundation techniques (the spec bank, the retrieval ladder, worked example fading) compound but take three weeks to show up in a score. Exam technique techniques (predicting the mark scheme, command words, self marking) show up in the very next paper. If you teach four foundation weeks in a row, month one feels like homework. Alternating means every fortnight contains one thing that pays this week and one thing that pays in March.

Each session below gives you: the idea, the line to say out loud, what you teach in the thirty minute block, what they leave with, the number that moves, and why it is not basic.

## Block A, sessions 2 to 5: make the content findable and the marks visible

### Session 2: The spec is the question bank

**The idea.** Examiners can only ask what is on the specification, and the spec is written in verbs. Convert every bullet into a question and you have the complete examinable universe for that topic, as a finite, tickable list.

**The line.** "Everything you will ever be asked is on four pages, and you have never read them. Let's turn them into questions, and then revision stops being infinite."

**What you teach.** Open their spec live on screen. Take one topic. Convert the bullets together: "describe the structure of X" is already a question, "understand how Y affects Z" becomes "how does Y affect Z, and why". Show them a four page spec section becoming roughly forty questions. Then have them RAG rate the forty in real time, cold. The red ones are the entire revision plan for that topic.

**What they leave with.** One topic converted, and the rule for doing the rest. This bank is the spine of everything afterwards: blurting runs off it, retrieval runs off it, the mock run-in runs off it.

**The number.** Questions generated, and the percentage they can answer cold. Track the cold percentage per topic from here to the exam.

**Why it is not basic.** Almost no student has opened the spec. The psychological effect is the point: revision stops feeling infinite and starts being a list with an end.

### Session 3: Predict the mark scheme before you answer

**The idea.** Before writing a word, the student writes the bullet points they think earn the marks. Then they answer. Then they check. Most lost marks are structural, not knowledge.

**The line.** "A six mark question wants six things. Before you write a sentence, tell me what the six things are. If you cannot name them, you are writing and hoping."

**What you teach.** Take three questions from their own paper. For each: read it, cover the answer space, write the predicted mark points as a numbered list, then compare to the real scheme. Count the hit rate. Show them the pattern in the gaps, which is almost always a missing "why" or a missing comparison. Then teach the rule: mark allocation tells you how many distinct points to make, and the command word tells you what shape each one takes.

**What they leave with.** The predict-then-answer routine, applied to every long question from now on.

**The number.** Predicted mark points hit, as a percentage of the real scheme. Expect 40 to 50 percent in week one and 75 plus by week six.

**Why it is not basic.** It converts a vague instruction (read the mark scheme) into a testable skill with a score attached, and it pays off on the very next paper.

### Session 4: The retrieval ladder

**The idea.** There are three rungs of retrieval: free recall (blank page, nothing given), cued recall (a prompt or heading), and recognition (flashcard, multiple choice). Exams sit at rung one and two. Almost every student revises at rung three and mistakes it for knowing.

**The line.** "Flashcards are the bottom rung. The exam is the top rung. Are you someone who has been practising at the bottom and wondering why the top feels impossible?"

**What you teach.** Run the same topic at all three rungs live, in this order: blank page first, then with headings, then with their flashcards. Let them feel the cliff between rung three and rung one. Then set the rule: every topic gets rated by the highest rung they can do, and revision means climbing a rung, not repeating one.

**What they leave with.** A rung rating on every topic in their weakest subject, and the rule that a topic is not finished until it is rung one.

**The number.** Topics at rung one, counted weekly. This replaces vague confidence ratings with something honest.

**Why it is not basic.** It gives them language for the exact failure they experience in exams and cannot explain, which is knowing something in the room and losing it on the page.

### Session 5: Command word drills, plus the month one re-run

**The idea.** State, describe, explain, compare, evaluate, justify, calculate. Each one wants a different shape of answer from identical content. Students answer the topic, not the question.

**The line.** "Same content, six different questions, six different answers. If you write the same paragraph every time, five of those six are wrong."

**What you teach.** Pick one topic they know well. Write six command words on screen. Have them answer the same topic six ways, out loud, in about ninety seconds each. Correct the shape, never the content. Then build their command word card: what each verb demands, in their own words, on one side of A4.

**Also this session: the first four tier re-run.** Fresh paper, same four buckets, bars side by side with session 1. Four weeks is long enough to move Exam technique measurably and short enough that they have not forgotten the baseline.

**What they leave with.** The command word card, and their first progress chart.

**The number.** The four tier split, run two. Plus marks lost to wording specifically.

**Why it is not basic.** Most students have been told command words matter and have never been made to produce six answers in ten minutes. Doing it out loud is what installs it.

## Block B, sessions 6 to 9: make practice honest and transfer possible

### Session 6: Interleaving and the mixed set

**The idea.** Practising one topic in a block feels productive and does not train the hardest exam skill, which is working out which knowledge a question wants. Mixed sets feel worse and score better.

**The line.** "In the exam nothing tells you which topic the question is from. If you have only ever practised in blocks, that is a skill you have never trained once."

**What you teach.** Build a mixed set live: ten questions pulled across four topics, shuffled. Have them do three on the call. Watch them hesitate before starting, and name that hesitation as the skill being trained. Warn them honestly that their scores will dip for a fortnight and then overtake, because that dip is where students abandon the method.

**What they leave with.** One mixed set per subject per week, replacing one blocked session.

**The number.** Mixed set score, tracked separately from blocked practice. Expect a dip then a climb.

**Why it is not basic.** The pre warning about the dip is what makes it survive contact with a demotivated Tuesday.

### Session 7: Self marking calibration

**The idea.** Students over mark themselves by twenty to thirty percent. If their self marking is generous, every practice paper they have ever done has been telling them a lie, and no amount of practice fixes it.

**The line.** "If you mark yourself kindly, every paper you do is lying to you. We are going to find out how kind you are, in numbers."

**What you teach.** They mark one of their own answers against the scheme. You mark the same answer. Compare. The gap is the calibration error. Then teach the three rules of harsh marking: the mark point must be present in words not implied, a vague phrase scores zero even when the idea is right, and if you are arguing for the mark you have not earned it.

**What they leave with.** Their calibration gap as a number, and the harsh marking rules on a card.

**The number.** Self mark minus real mark, tracked monthly. Target is within one mark on a six mark question.

**Why it is not basic.** It is a force multiplier: it makes every hour of independent practice they do for the rest of the year honest. And it is a genuinely surprising number for the student, which makes it memorable.

### Session 8: Worked example fading

**The idea.** For anything with a method (calculations, mechanisms, structured derivations), going straight from reading a worked solution to attempting one unaided is too big a jump. Fade the support one step at a time.

**The line.** "You read the worked answer, it made total sense, and then you could not do the next one. That is not you being slow. You skipped three rungs."

**What you teach.** Take one worked solution from their material. Pass one: they study it and explain each step's purpose out loud. Pass two: you blank the final step, they complete it. Pass three: blank the last two steps. Pass four: unaided, new numbers. Do two full cycles on the call so they feel the ladder.

**What they leave with.** The fading routine for every method based topic, and the rule that a worked example is a four pass resource, not a read.

**The number.** Passes needed to reach unaided, per topic. It falls over time and that fall is visible.

**Why it is not basic.** It is a specific answer to the most common complaint in maths and chemistry, which is "I understand it when I watch it and freeze when I try it".

### Session 9: Question decomposition, and the month two re-run

**The idea.** Train them to name the spec point behind any question within ten seconds. Examiners have a finite bank of things to ask and an infinite number of costumes to dress them in.

**The line.** "This question is not new. It is the same spec point in a different jacket. Your job in the first ten seconds is to work out which jacket."

**What you teach.** Take eight unseen questions. For each, they do not answer, they only say which spec point it is testing and what the first move is. Eight questions in fifteen minutes. Then show them two questions from different years that are the same spec point in different contexts, side by side.

**Also this session: four tier re-run number three,** plus the first proper trend line across three data points.

**What they leave with.** The ten second decode habit, and three data points on their chart.

**The number.** Decode accuracy out of eight, and time to decode.

**Why it is not basic.** It directly attacks the "the exam was nothing like the past papers" complaint, which is nearly always a failure to recognise a familiar spec point in unfamiliar clothing.

## Block C, sessions 10 to 13: the B to A jump

### Session 10: The first move library

**The idea.** Marks are lost to freezing, not only to not knowing. For each recurring question type, decide in advance what the first line on the page is.

**The line.** "You do not need to know the whole answer to start. You need to know the first line. Let's write the first line for every question type you will meet."

**What you teach.** List their six or seven recurring question types. For each, agree the opening move: define the term, write the equation, label the axes, state the null hypothesis, name the two things being compared. Then drill: you read a question, they say the first move, five seconds, no answering.

**What they leave with.** The first move library, one page per subject, which grows all year.

**The number.** Blank starts, which is questions where they stalled before writing anything. This should hit zero.

**Why it is not basic.** It turns exam paralysis into a lookup, and it is the single fastest intervention for a student who says they panic.

### Session 11: Examiner report mining

**The idea.** Exam boards publish, free, a document that says exactly what students got wrong last year and why. Almost nobody reads it. It is the closest thing to seeing the marking from the inside.

**The line.** "The board publishes a document every year telling you precisely how students lose marks on this paper. It is free, it is about ten pages, and nobody in your year has opened it."

**What you teach.** Download one together on the call. Show them the shape: candidates frequently confused X with Y, few candidates gained the third mark, stronger responses referred to Z. Teach the mining method: pull out every sentence beginning with a candidate behaviour, turn each into a rule for themselves, and add the rules to their phrase bank and error log.

**What they leave with.** One report mined into eight to ten personal rules.

**The number.** Rules extracted, and how many of those errors they then make on the next paper.

**Why it is not basic.** It is high yield, free, requires zero subject knowledge from you, and feels like insider information, which is exactly the feeling that keeps a student on a retainer.

### Session 12: The return schedule that adjusts itself

**The idea.** Spacing fails when it relies on memory or motivation. A topic's next review date should be set by how well the last retrieval went, not by a fixed calendar.

**The line.** "Do not decide when to come back to a topic. Let the topic decide, based on how badly it went."

**What you teach.** The three lane system: a topic retrieved badly returns in two days, patchily in seven, cleanly in twenty one. Build it live from their rung ratings. Then the weekly sweep: every Sunday, ten minutes, move topics between lanes based on the week's retrievals.

**What they leave with.** Their whole topic list sorted into three dated lanes, and the Sunday sweep as a ritual.

**The number.** Topics in the two day lane, which should shrink, and topics surviving a twenty one day gap, which should grow.

**Why it is not basic.** It replaces a fixed timetable, which every student abandons by week three, with a system that self corrects and takes ten minutes a week.

### Session 13: The unfamiliar context drill, and the month three re-run

**The idea.** The B to A jump is transfer: applying a known spec point to a context they have never seen. It is trainable and almost nobody trains it.

**The line.** "An A student and a B student often know the same content. The difference is what happens when the question puts it somewhere new."

**What you teach.** Pick one spec point they are solid on. Find five questions across years and boards that test it in five different contexts. Work three on the call. After each, ask the same question: what stayed the same, what changed, and which part of the change was cosmetic. Teach them to strip the context and find the physics or the chemistry underneath.

**Also: four tier re-run number four.** By now Knowledge and Exam technique should both be visibly down and the remaining loss should be concentrating in Application, which is exactly the story to tell the parent this month.

**What they leave with.** The strip the context routine, and four data points showing where the loss has moved.

**The number.** Score on first attempt at an unfamiliar context, tracked separately.

**Why it is not basic.** It is the specific, teachable mechanism behind a grade jump that most tutoring treats as mysterious.

## Block D, sessions 14 to 17: performance under pressure

### Session 14: The two pass paper and timing checkpoints

**The idea.** Marks are lost to paper strategy: too long on question three, nothing written for question eleven. Teach the sweep first, then the return.

**The line.** "You are not marked on the order you answer in. The most expensive thing in that room is twelve minutes on a question you were never going to get."

**What you teach.** Marks per minute for their specific paper. Pass one: answer everything you can do immediately, flag the rest. Pass two: return to the flagged ones with the time that remains. The sixty second rule: stuck for a minute, flag and move. Checkpoints written on the paper before starting: by question eight I should be at minute thirty five. Run one section live with checkpoints.

**What they leave with.** A checkpoint card per paper, and the two pass habit.

**The number.** Questions left blank, which should be zero, and time used against time allowed.

**Why it is not basic.** Students are told to manage time and never shown a mechanism. The checkpoint card is a mechanism.

### Session 15: Teach it back, on tape

**The idea.** Explaining out loud to an imaginary beginner exposes every place they are hand waving. Recording it and listening back is what makes the hand waving audible to them.

**The line.** "Record three minutes explaining this topic to someone in Year 10. Then listen back and count how many times you say 'sort of' or 'something like'. Every one of those is a gap you did not know you had."

**What you teach.** They record three minutes on the call. Play it back together. Mark the hedges, the skipped steps, the moments they reach for the notes. Then re record after fixing only the flagged parts, and compare.

**What they leave with.** The voice note routine, one topic a week, and a hedge count.

**The number.** Hedges per three minutes, which falls fast and visibly.

**Why it is not basic.** It is retrieval, self assessment and precision language in one exercise, it takes six minutes, and the before and after in their own voice is unusually persuasive.

### Session 16: The mock run-in protocol

**The idea.** The three weeks before a mock, run properly, are the whole exam run-in in miniature. Learning it in December means April is a repeat rather than a first attempt.

**The line.** "We are going to run December exactly the way we will run May. This is the rehearsal, and the rehearsal is the point."

**What you teach.** Build the coverage map: every topic gets a final pass, ordered by rung rating, weakest first. Two full mixed papers placed. The last forty eight hours as retrieval only, nothing new. Then the written prediction: they write down the mark they expect, sealed, to compare afterwards. Calibration is a skill and this is how you measure it.

**What they leave with.** A dated run-in plan and a written prediction.

**The number.** Predicted mark against actual. Then the mock result itself.

**Why it is not basic.** The prediction turns a mock from a verdict into an experiment, which takes most of the fear out of it.

### Session 17: The mock autopsy, and the four month review

**The idea.** The mock is the richest data of the whole programme. Treat it as a paper, not a grade.

**The line.** "The grade is the least interesting thing on this paper. Let's find out where the marks actually went."

**What you teach.** Full four tier analysis on the mock, run five. Then the grid: mark loss by tier down one axis, by topic across the other. The three fixes that would have taken them over the next boundary, each with the marks it is worth. Then the four month review: all five charts side by side, the assets they now own, and what the next block adds.

**What they leave with.** The five point chart, the three named fixes, and a reason to carry on that they worked out themselves.

**The number.** Everything, together, for the first time.

**Why it is not basic.** This is the session that renews the retainer, and it renews it with evidence rather than persuasion. Send the parent the same chart the same day.

Twelve more teaches, so nothing repeats before month twelve. Pull them in the order their data asks for.

## Part two: the other things to focus on

**The chart is the product, so make it look like one.** Build a simple four bar chart, same axes every time, with the date on it. Send it to the parent monthly. Five months of those side by side is the most persuasive sales asset this business will ever own, and it is a by product of the teaching you already do.

**Name the method.** Right now you have a four tier analysis with no name that a student can repeat to a friend. Give it one, use it in every session, put it on the site. A student who can say "we ran my KRE split and found I was losing thirty percent on exam technique" is doing your marketing for you.

**Adherence is a teachable topic, and you are not tracking it.** The best technique in the world fails if it is not run on a Tuesday evening. Every session should start with what they actually did, as a count, not a feeling, and a student who does under half of the between session work needs a session about that rather than the next technique. Put a done percentage on the scorecard.

**Do not drift into subject content.** It will be tempting when a student is stuck and you can see the answer. The moment you explain a topic, you become a subject tutor who does not know their spec, and the whole positioning goes. Send them to the teacher, the textbook or a Subject Accelerator, and say why out loud. Saying it out loud actually raises trust rather than lowering it.

**Record each teach once, the week you first give it.** Twelve of the sixteen above are identical for every student. A ten minute recording of each becomes the classroom for the Skool room later, and it is the difference between fifteen students and thirty. Record them as you go rather than as a project.

**Plan the month four conversation instead of letting it happen.** Session 17 is where the minimum term ends. Go into it with the five charts, the three named fixes and the specific next block on the table. The ask is never "do you want to continue", it is "here is what the next three months are for, and here is what we would be working on".

**Recruit Year 12s for the year plus story.** Year 13 has a hard June ceiling. A Year 12 who starts in January runs to the following summer and often into their exam year, which is eighteen months. That is where the annual value lives, and it should shape who you say yes to.

**One shared page per student.** Everything above produces artefacts, and right now they live in his notebook and your head. One shared document per student, holding the chart, the question bank, the error log, the first move library and the phrase bank, means the progress is visible between sessions and the student can see the pile growing. That visible pile is what makes month seven feel like continuing rather than renewing.
